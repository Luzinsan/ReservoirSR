\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter details the sequential experimental methodology developed to reconstruct high-fidelity multiphase flow states in fractured-porous media. The research framework is structured as a progressive validation of deep learning hypotheses, transitioning from baseline regression architectures to advanced physics-informed adversarial networks. 

\section{Data Generation Pipeline}
\label{sec:framework_data}

The foundation of this research relies on a mathematically rigorous definition of the physical system and its underlying operational assumptions. The computational domain represents a two-dimensional vertical cross-section of a Type II fractured-porous reservoir. The mathematical formulation assumes an incompressible solid rock matrix but slightly compressible fluid phases. This compressibility difference activates the elastic cross-flow mechanism, allowing fluid to transfer between the porous matrix blocks and the highly conductive fracture network under transient pressure variations. Furthermore, the macroscopic effect of capillary pressure between the water and oil phases is considered mathematically negligible, representing a standard simplification for highly permeable fracture systems dominated by viscous forces.

To generate the synthetic dataset, a custom numerical simulator was utilized. The physical behavior of the reservoir is governed by a system of non-linear parabolic partial differential equations. The simulator resolves this coupled system using an implicit finite-difference mathematical scheme. The resulting algebraic system for the global pressure field is calculated through a highly stable five-point summary sweep algorithm, followed by an upwind differencing update for the phase saturations.

The data generation process requires strict boundary intervals for all variables to ensure both thermodynamic validity and computational feasibility. These bounds represent the optimal intersection between physical reality and numerical stability, preventing extreme stiffness in the implicit solver. The petrophysical and fluid parameters were sampled according to the following specifications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Permeability Sampling:} The absolute permeability of matrix blocks, denoted as $AKB$, and fractures, denoted as $AKT$, are uniformly sampled within the interval from 10 to 1000 mD. A sampling step of 50 mD is utilized to provide a representative distribution of heterogeneous configurations.
    \item \textbf{Porosity Sampling:} The matrix porosity $VMB$ and fracture porosity $VMT$ are sampled from the interval between 0.05 and 0.30 with a constant increment of 0.02. 
    \item \textbf{Saturation Constraints:} The mobile fluid phases are bounded by the irreducible water saturations $SNB$ and $SNT$, set at 0.1, and the maximum water saturations $SVB$ and $SVT$, set at 0.9.
    \item \textbf{Fluid Properties:} The water viscosity is fixed at 1.0 cP, while the reference oil viscosity is set at 50.0 cP. The non-Newtonian breakdown viscosity, represented by $MU\_pazp$, is parameterized at 8.0 cP to model non-linear mobility ratios during the displacement process.
    \item \textbf{Grid Resolution and Scaling:} The low-resolution computational grids are fixed at $25 \times 25$ cells. To maintain a constant spatial upscaling factor of $x4$, the independent high-resolution targets are computed on a $100 \times 100$ mesh.
\end{itemize}

The dataset generation pipeline was designed for parallel execution to accommodate the iterative nature of the numerical solver. While the five-point summary sweep algorithm scales linearly with the number of grid cells, the total computational load increases significantly because the system must reach a pressure residual $EPSP$ below $10^{-6}$ and involves a temporal sub-layering factor $N\_Dr$ of 10. The continuous temporal evolution is recorded at fixed observation intervals of 30 days, governed by the $DSO$ parameter, up to a maximum simulation time of 1500 days. This yields exactly 50 sequential temporal snapshots per simulation. By generating 500 independent reservoir configurations, a total training population of 25000 spatial state pairs was obtained. During the deep learning optimization phase, the models are trained utilizing mini-batches of 16 spatial tensor pairs to ensure stable gradient descent.

The thermodynamic state of the reservoir at any discrete time step is mapped into a defined multi-variable tensor. Specifically, the input to the neural network is structured as a physical tensor denoted by $X$ with dimensions $3 \times H \times W$, comprising the following components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Global Pressure:} Represented by the variable $P$ in the first channel, this field dictates the total macroscopic flow velocity. It is initialized at 15.0 MPa and monitored within a range up to 50.0 MPa.
    \item \textbf{Fracture Saturation:} Encoded as $ST$ in the second channel, this variable represents the rapid convective displacement fronts and is constrained by the saturation limits defined in the data generation stage.
    \item \textbf{Matrix Saturation:} Encoded as $SB$ in the third channel, this variable reflects the slow elastic fluid exchange occurring within the isolated blocks.
\end{itemize}

Because these variables operate on vastly different numerical scales, a channel-wise min-max normalization procedure is applied to scale all fields into a uniform unit interval. This specific normalization technique is selected over standard score normalization to preserve the strict physical boundaries of the saturation fields and maintain the relative magnitude of pressure gradients, which is essential for the thermodynamic consistency of the model.

The ultimate objective of this surrogate modeling approach is to minimize inference latency while maintaining high-resolution fidelity. The practical viability of the methodology is evaluated by comparing the execution time of the high-resolution numerical solver against the combined duration of the coarse-grid simulation and the subsequent neural network forward pass. To ensure computational adequacy, the grid resolutions were selected such that the entire dataset could be generated within a reasonable timeframe on a standard workstation. Machine learning latency is rigorously measured in two distinct environments, specifically the native PyTorch evaluation mode and an optimized computational graph executed via the ONNX Runtime engine.


\section{Regression Baselines}
\label{sec:regression_baselines}

To test the hypothesis regarding the limitations of point-wise optimization, the methodology establishes a highly optimized external regression baseline utilizing the Multi-scale Deep Super-Resolution architecture. While this model is theoretically capable of handling multiple upscaling factors, the current implementation is strictly configured for a constant magnification of four. The forward pass of the MDSR model is defined by the following sequential transformations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial Feature Extraction (Head):} The network receives the normalized low-resolution state tensor $X_{LR}$ with dimensions of $3 \times 25 \times 25$. A scale-specific convolutional layer utilizing 64 filters of size $3 \times 3$ and a stride of 1 extracts shallow spatial features. This transformation expands the channel depth while maintaining the spatial resolution, resulting in a feature tensor $X_{head} \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 25 \times 25}$.
    \item \textbf{Shared Residual Body:} The core of the network consists of 80 simplified residual blocks. These blocks are considered shared because they process features independently of the final upscaling factor. The total feature depth of 64 channels is preserved throughout this stage, and the output tensor $X_{body}$ maintains the shape of $\mathbb{R}^{64 \times 25 \times 25}$.
    \item \textbf{Simplified Residual Block Structure:} Each of the 80 blocks is designed without batch normalization layers to maximize range flexibility for the physical variables. The internal sequence of operations within a single block includes:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item A convolutional layer with 64 filters, a $3 \times 3$ kernel, and a padding of 1.
        \item A Rectified Linear Unit activation function.
        \item A second convolutional layer with identical parameters to the first.
        \item A constant residual scaling layer that multiplies the output by a factor of 0.1 to maintain training stability in the absence of normalization.
        \item An element-wise identity addition operation that fuses the scaled residual features with the original block input.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Upsampling Module (Tail):} For the target $x4$ upscaling task, a scale-specific tail module is deployed. A convolutional layer first expands the channel count from 64 to 1024, representing the $64 \times r^2$ projection. This is immediately followed by a PixelShuffle operator that rearranges the high-dimensional channel elements into the spatial domain. This process increases the spatial resolution to $100 \times 100$ grid cells, resulting in an upsampled feature tensor $X_{up} \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 100 \times 100}$.
    \item \textbf{Global State Reconstruction:} A final convolutional layer with 3 filters and a $3 \times 3$ kernel size projects the deep features back into the physical variable space. This concludes the forward pass, outputting the high-resolution prediction $X_{HR} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 100 \times 100}$.
\end{itemize}

The selection of this architecture is predicated on several technical advantages beyond the mere exclusion of normalization layers. A primary benefit is the scale-invariant feature extraction capability provided by the shared residual body. By training on the specific upscaling factor, the model learns fundamental spatial correlations between the global pressure and saturation fields that represent the underlying physics of fluid transport rather than resolution-specific numerical artifacts. Furthermore, the substantial depth of eighty residual blocks provides a massive receptive field. This extensive spatial coverage is essential for capturing the long-range connectivity of fracture networks across the studied computational domain. From a computational perspective, the post-upsampling strategy minimizes the total count of floating-point operations by maintaining the feature maps in the low-dimensional space for the majority of the forward pass. This efficiency allows the architecture to achieve high throughput during the operational phase while maintaining a robust numerical stability.

Nevertheless, the methodology acknowledges certain drawbacks inherent to any model optimized strictly for point-wise error minimization. While the MDSR architecture establishes an upper bound for mathematical fidelity metrics such as the peak signal-to-noise ratio, it remains vulnerable to the regression-to-the-mean effect. In the context of multiphase flow, this manifests as the artificial smoothing of sharp saturation discontinuities and a significant loss of energy in the high-frequency spectral domain. These limitations confirm that while the regression-based baseline is suitable for identifying the general topology of the flow, it is insufficient for resolving the fine-scale instabilities necessary for accurate engineering forecasts.




While the multi-scale architecture provides a robust external benchmark, validating the subsequent network interpolation hypothesis requires an internal regression baseline possessing a topological structure absolutely identical to the intended generative model. Therefore, a secondary regression baseline is constructed utilizing a deep network of Residual-in-Residual Dense Blocks. This internal baseline is designed to establish a high-precision numerical reference that can later be fused with adversarial weights. The stage-wise topological transformations of this architecture are defined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial Feature Mapping:} The network receives the normalized low-resolution state tensor $X_{LR} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 25 \times 25}$. A first convolutional layer with 64 filters and a $3 \times 3$ kernel size extracts shallow features, outputting a tensor $X_{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 25 \times 25}$.
    \item \textbf{RRDB Backbone Trunk:} The core of the model consists of 23 sequential RRDB units. Unlike simple residual blocks, each RRDB possesses a hierarchical architecture where three dense blocks are combined with a residual connection. Within each of these dense blocks, five convolutional layers are arranged such that each layer receives the feature maps of all preceding layers as input. This dense connectivity results in a growth rate of 32 channels per internal layer, though the external output of each RRDB unit is projected back to 64 channels. The tensor shape remains constant at $\mathbb{R}^{64 \times 25 \times 25}$ throughout this entire backbone.
    \item \textbf{Global Residual Learning:} A global skip connection adds the initial shallow features from $X_{ head}$ to the output of the final RRDB unit. This operation ensures that low-frequency pressure and saturation information is preserved before the spatial resolution is increased.
    \item \textbf{Sub-Pixel Upsampling Stage:} The upsampling module performs two sequential magnification steps. Each step involves a convolutional layer that expands the channel depth followed by a PixelShuffle operator to double the spatial resolution. The first step transforms the tensor to $\mathbb{R}^{64 \times 50 \times 50}$, and the second step yields a deep spatial representation of shape $\mathbb{R}^{64 \times 100 \times 100}$.
    \item \textbf{Target State Projection:} A final convolutional layer collapses the 64 feature channels back into the three fundamental physical variables. The resulting output $X_{HR} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 100 \times 100}$ represents the high-resolution state prediction.
\end{itemize}

The deployment of the RRDB architecture introduces a significantly higher model capacity compared to the MDSR baseline. By utilizing dense connections within each residual block, the network maximizes the information flow and facilitates the learning of more complex, non-linear spatial hierarchies. This capacity is particularly advantageous for resolving the intricate geometries of high-permeability channels where flow patterns exhibit abrupt transitions. Furthermore, the absence of batch normalization layers in this architecture prevents the loss of absolute numerical range fidelity, which is critical for maintaining the physical meaning of pressure gradients.

However, the methodology acknowledges that the increased complexity of RRDB units leads to a higher computational burden. The dense concatenations within each block increase the total number of floating-point operations and the memory footprint during training. Compared to the MDSR architecture, which focuses on parameter efficiency through multi-scale sharing, the RRDB-based model prioritizes feature extraction depth. In terms of expected performance, this baseline is anticipated to achieve point-wise accuracy scores comparable to MDSR while providing the necessary topological foundation for subsequent adversarial refinement. A primary disadvantage of this model remains the regression-to-the-mean effect common to all Mean Squared Error optimization tasks. Consequently, while the RRDB regression baseline yields high signal stability and low average error, it is theoretically insufficient for recovering the high-frequency displacement fronts that characterize unstable multiphase filtration.


\section{The Adversarial Framework}
\label{sec:adversarial_protocol}

To resolve the high-frequency smoothing effect inherent to point-wise regression, the methodology transitions to the Enhanced Super-Resolution Generative Adversarial Network framework, which was theoretically introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:gan_revolution}. The fundamental justification for this transition lies in the capacity of the adversarial objective to learn the underlying distribution of the high-resolution state manifold rather than a deterministic average. While Mean Squared Error optimization leads to a regression-to-the-mean effect, the adversarial game forces the generator to synthesize sharp discontinuities and instabilities by penalizing outputs that deviate from the statistical properties of the reference physical fields.

The implemented ESRGAN architecture consists of two primary sub-networks: a generator and a relativistic average discriminator. The structural details and layer configurations are defined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generator Architecture (RRDBNet):} The generator utilizes the 23-block RRDB topology defined in Section~\ref{sec:regression_baselines}. With 64 internal feature channels and a growth rate of 32 within each dense block, the generator contains approximately 16.7 million trainable parameters. This high capacity is essential for resolving the coupled dynamics of the global pressure and dual saturation fields.
    \item \textbf{Relativistic Average Discriminator:} This sub-network is designed to estimate the relative probability that the high-resolution ground truth is more realistic than the generated state. The architecture comprises a VGG-style sequential feature contraction pipeline:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Input Reception:} The network accepts a high-resolution tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 100 \times 100}$ representing the three physical variables.
        \item \textbf{Initial Feature Mapping:} A convolutional layer with 64 filters and a $3 \times 3$ kernel size transforms the input into a feature map of shape $\mathbb{R}^{64 \times 100 \times 100}$.
        \item \textbf{Spatial Contraction Block 1:} A series of two convolutions increases the depth to 128 channels. A stride of 2 is applied to reduce the spatial resolution, outputting a tensor of shape $\mathbb{R}^{128 \times 50 \times 50}$.
        \item \textbf{Spatial Contraction Block 2:} The representation is expanded to 256 channels through two convolutional stages. A second strided operation reduces the dimensions to a shape of $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times 25 \times 25}$.
        \item \textbf{Spatial Contraction Block 3:} The channel count is doubled to 512. The spatial grid is contracted to a feature map of shape $\mathbb{R}^{512 \times 12 \times 12}$.
        \item \textbf{Global Feature Aggregation:} A concluding convolutional layer with a stride of 2 yields a deep bottleneck representation of shape $\mathbb{R}^{512 \times 6 \times 6}$.
        \item \textbf{Classification Head:} The spatial tensor is flattened into a vector of 18,432 nodes and processed by two dense linear layers. The final output is a single scalar value representing the relative realism probability.
    \end{enumerate}
    This sub-network accounts for approximately 20.3 million parameters, totaling a model complexity of roughly 37 million parameters for the complete framework.
    \item \textbf{Engineering Application of VGG-19:} To calculate the perceptual loss, a pre-trained VGG-19 network is utilized as a frozen feature extractor. From an engineering perspective, since the VGG architecture is designed for three-channel RGB images, the normalized physical tensor is treated as a pseudo-color representation. Features are extracted before the activation layers, specifically from the fifth convolutional layer of the fourth block, denoted as convolution 4-5. This choice ensures that the loss function captures the raw structural gradients of the pressure and saturation fronts without the non-linear distortions introduced by the ReLU functions.
\end{itemize}

To rigorously evaluate the convergence and stability of this framework, two distinct experimental protocols are implemented. The first experiment involves training the complete GAN architecture from a random weight initialization. Due to the extreme non-linearity of the three-channel multiphase flow tensor, it is anticipated that this uninitialized approach will suffer from mode collapse, where the generator produces a limited set of physically incorrect patterns to satisfy the discriminator. 

To resolve this instability, a second experiment utilizes a pre-training initialization strategy. In this protocol, the generator is first optimized for 100,000 iterations using only the Mean Squared Error loss. The adversarial training then proceeds by utilizing these pre-trained weights as the starting point. This strategy allows the model to first establish a correct macroscopic topology of the pressure field before refining the high-frequency boundaries of the water breakthrough fronts. The learning rates for the generator and discriminator are decoupled, with the discriminator utilizing a lower rate to prevent it from overwhelming the generator during the initial phases of adversarial optimization.

The expected performance of the ESRGAN framework is a significant improvement in structural metrics such as SSIM and LPIPS, alongside a matching of the high-frequency Fourier energy density. However, a primary engineering challenge is the potential introduction of high-frequency computational noise, which can degrade point-wise signal stability. Furthermore, the increased depth and dense connections of the RRDB units result in a higher computational cost during the training phase, although the inference latency remains within acceptable bounds for surrogate modeling applications. By implementing these two protocols, the research aims to identify the optimal balance between training stability and the accurate resolution of fine-scale hydrodynamic instabilities.





\section{Dual-System Physics-Informed Regularization}
\label{sec:physics_regularization}

To suppress the generation of thermodynamically impossible states, a novel physics-informed regularization module was derived and integrated into the adversarial objective function. The fundamental necessity for this module arises from the inability of standard convolutional layers to inherently respect the conservation of mass. In dual-porosity systems, this issue is further complicated by the interaction between two overlapping continua. To ensure that the generative network adheres to the underlying physics of Type II reservoirs, the loss function must be constrained by the integral form of the governing equations established in Section~\ref{sec:theoretical_foundations}.

The mathematical derivation of the proposed loss function begins with the local mass conservation equations for the fracture network and the matrix blocks, denoted by Equations~\ref{eq:fpor_frac_sat} and \ref{eq:fpor_block_sat}. These equations describe the rate of change of fluid volume within each medium. A critical property of the single-level Type II model is the coupling term representing the elastic mass exchange between the media. In the local formulation, this term appears with opposite signs in the fracture and matrix equations. By summing these two partial differential equations, the internal transfer term is mathematically eliminated:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial \tau} (m S + \bar{m} \bar{S}) + \text{div}(f \mathbf{V}) + \text{Source Terms} = 0,
\end{equation}
where $m$ and $\bar{m}$ represent the porosities of the fractures and matrix blocks, while $S$ and $\bar{S}$ denote their respective water saturations. This combined equation describes the total conservation of the water phase across the entire dual-porosity volume element.

Integrating this relationship over the entire computational domain $\Omega$ provides a global conservation law. According to the divergence theorem, the integral of the divergence term corresponds to the fluid flux across the external boundaries. Since the super-resolution task aims to reconstruct a high-resolution state from a coarse-grid simulation at the same discrete time step, the boundary fluxes and source terms are assumed to be implicitly preserved in the coarse-grid solution. Consequently, the total fluid volume present in the high-resolution prediction must strictly match the total fluid volume calculated from the coarse-grid input. This leads to the definition of the Dual-System Material Balance Error, formulated as a differentiable loss penalty:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{mass} = \left( \sum_{i,j} V_{i,j} (m_{i,j} S_{i,j}^{SR} + \bar{m}_{i,j} \bar{S}_{i,j}^{SR}) - \sum_{k,l} \mathcal{V}_{k,l} (\Phi_{k,l} S_{k,l}^{LR} + \bar{\Phi}_{k,l} \bar{S}_{k,l}^{LR}) \right)^2.
\end{equation}
In this expression, the variables $V$ and $m$ correspond to the cell volume and fracture porosity on the fine grid, while $\mathcal{V}$ and $\Phi$ represent the same properties on the coarse mesh. The predicted high-resolution fields are denoted by the superscript $SR$, and the input fields are marked as $LR$.

This formulation is expected to be effective because it forces the generator to maintain the integral mass balance across the three-channel tensor. Specifically, it prevents the discriminator from encouraging the generator to synthesize sharp displacement fronts by "hallucinating" fluid volumes that do not exist in the coarse-grid physics. By coupling the fracture and matrix channels within a single loss term, the model is compelled to learn the correct volumetric relationship between the two media. 

However, several limitations and conditions for the applicability of this regularization must be acknowledged. This integral loss assumes that the coarse-grid numerical solver provides a physically valid reference for the total fluid inventory. If the coarse simulation suffers from extreme mass balance errors due to poor convergence or excessive time-stepping, the neural network will be regularized toward an incorrect physical state. Furthermore, this specific form of the mass balance loss is most effective when the boundary conditions are static or slowly varying. In cases of rapid changes in injection or production rates between snapshots, additional temporal derivative terms would be required to maintain strict consistency. Finally, because adversarial gradients and physical penalty gradients often operate at conflicting numerical scales, the total objective function utilizes a dynamic weighting parameter to prevent the mass conservation constraint from over-smoothing the structural details resolved by the perceptual loss.


\section{Network Interpolation Mechanism}
\label{sec:network_interpolation}

The final phase of the methodology addresses the fundamental trade-off between the localized signal stability of the regression baseline and the structural sharpness of the physics-informed adversarial model. This trade-off, often referred to as the perception-distortion compromise, is resolved through the mathematical fusion of learned parameters. Because both the internal regression baseline and the final generative model share an identical structural topology, their respective parameter sets denoted by $\theta^{MSE}$ and $\theta^{GAN}$ exist within the same dimensionality of the weight space.

The theoretical justification for this approach is based on the phenomenon of linear mode connectivity in deep neural networks. Recent studies in optimization landscapes suggest that different minima of the same network architecture, when originating from the same pre-trained state, are often connected by a path of low loss. By initializing the adversarial training with the optimal weights of the regression model, the methodology ensures that the two resulting parameter sets remain within a coherent region of the manifold. This proximity allows for a continuous linear interpolation to compute a hybrid parameter set denoted by $\theta^{INTERP}$. The interpolated network state is governed by a continuous scalar coefficient represented by $\alpha$, which is bounded between zero and one:
\begin{equation}
    \theta^{INTERP} = (1 - \alpha)\theta^{MSE} + \alpha\theta^{GAN}.
\end{equation}
A systematic evaluation of the error landscape across the parameter $\alpha$ is required to identify the state that minimizes the composite evaluation criterion established in the objectives.

Several critical engineering nuances and limitations must be considered when implementing this mechanism. The primary indication for applying network interpolation is the presence of high-frequency computational noise or non-physical artifacts in the purely adversarial output. In such cases, the interpolation procedure effectively acts as a learned spatial regularizer. However, a significant prerequisite for the success of this method is the absolute topological identity of the models. Any structural discrepancy, such as a difference in the number of dense residual blocks or the growth rate of the layers, renders the parameter fusion mathematically impossible. 

Furthermore, the methodology requires a preliminary distance analysis of the weight tensors before interpolation. If the adversarial optimization causes the weights $\theta^{GAN}$ to diverge excessively from the regression starting point $\theta^{MSE}$, a linear transition may result in a phenomenon known as ghosting. This artifact manifests as a non-coherent superposition of two distinct physical states rather than a single unified flow pattern. To mitigate this risk, the training protocol utilizes a relatively low learning rate during the adversarial phase to preserve the underlying topological features learned during regression.
