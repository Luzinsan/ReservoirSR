\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

The literature review formulated three research questions and three corresponding hypotheses that govern the present work. This chapter translates those questions into a concrete experimental methodology. The chapter is organized along the same logical order in which the research questions are introduced: a methodological overview that connects the theory of Chapter~\ref{chap:lr} to the experimental design, the construction of the dataset, the choice of pixel-loss regression baselines, the design of the physics-informed adversarial framework, the design of the conditioning experiment, and the comparative analysis plan that integrates all three lines of investigation. Implementation-level details, including the specific numerical ranges, hyperparameter values, software stack, and training schedules, are deferred to Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

\section{Research Methodology Overview}
\label{sec:methodology_overview}

The methodology was designed as a sequence of three controlled experiments that progressively refine the surrogate model. Each experiment isolates one architectural or training decision and links it to a specific research question.

The first experiment establishes pixel-loss regression baselines using residual super-resolution backbones, as motivated by Section~\ref{subsec:dl_foundations}. The aim of this stage is to obtain a reference of point-wise reconstruction quality on the dual-porosity state tensor and to identify the limitations of pixel-loss optimization in the specific context of Type~II fractured-porous media. This experiment addresses the regression baseline component of RQ1.

The second experiment introduces adversarial training augmented with a physics-informed regularization term, as motivated by Sections~\ref{subsec:gan_revolution} and~\ref{sec:physics_sr}. The aim of this stage is to evaluate whether the perception-distortion trade-off identified in the broader super-resolution literature also applies to reservoir physics, and whether the proposed physics-informed regularizer mitigates the artefacts introduced by adversarial training. This experiment jointly addresses the adversarial component of RQ1 and the physics-informed component of RQ2.

The third experiment is conducted in parallel with the pixel-loss regression baselines and extends the MDSR backbone with feature-wise linear modulation conditioned on a vector of dynamic simulation scalars, as motivated by Section~\ref{subsec:conditioning}. The aim of this stage is to test whether the conditioning signal disambiguates plausible reconstructions corresponding to the same coarse-grid input under different operational regimes, independently of any adversarial refinement. This experiment addresses RQ3 and is reported in the present work as an exploratory study; its outcome and its implications for the surrogate design are summarized in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

The three experiments share a common dataset, a common evaluation framework, and a common inference pipeline, which allows a fair comparison of architectures and training objectives. The full experimental matrix is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:methodology_matrix}.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:methodology_dataset}

The construction of the training dataset is a methodological decision in its own right. The dataset must reflect the physical setting of Type~II fractured-porous media described in Section~\ref{sec:theoretical_foundations}, cover a sufficiently broad region of the parameter space to support generalization, and produce coarse-grid and fine-grid solutions in a manner consistent with the surrogate task.

The computational domain represents a two-dimensional vertical cross-section of a Type~II fractured-porous reservoir. The rock matrix is assumed incompressible, while the fluid phases are assumed slightly compressible. The difference in compressibility activates the elastic mass exchange between the matrix blocks and the highly conductive fracture network, which is the central physical mechanism described by Equation~\ref{eq:mass_transfer}. The macroscopic effect of capillary pressure between the water and the oil phases is treated as negligible, which is a standard simplification for highly permeable fracture systems dominated by viscous forces. The relative permeability of the fracture phase is assumed to depend linearly on saturation, while the relative permeability of the matrix phase follows a cubic relationship, in agreement with the governing system of Section~\ref{sec:theoretical_foundations}.

These assumptions delimit the regime in which the surrogate model is expected to operate. Configurations that violate them, such as settings with strong capillary effects or with significant rock compressibility, are out of scope for the present study and are documented as future work in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.

Within this physical scope, a central methodological decision concerns how the paired coarse-grid and fine-grid states are obtained. Two strategies are common in the super-resolution literature: direct downsampling of a fine-grid solution onto a coarse grid, and independent simulation on each grid. The first strategy is computationally cheap but methodologically inappropriate for the present task: the resulting coarse-grid field is a low-pass filtered version of the high-resolution reference and does not contain the numerical diffusion that arises from solving the governing equations on a coarse mesh. A surrogate trained on such pairs would learn to invert a blurring kernel rather than to recover lost physical information.

The present work therefore adopts the second strategy. For each scenario, the coarse-grid and fine-grid solutions are obtained by running the numerical solver independently on two distinct grid resolutions. The coarse-grid solution carries the authentic numerical diffusion that a coarse simulator would produce in practice, and the fine-grid solution carries the fine-scale physics that the surrogate model is expected to recover. The numerical solver, its discretization scheme, and the specific grid resolutions adopted in this study are documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

With the simulation strategy fixed, the next decision concerns the population of scenarios. To support generalization across operational and geological regimes, each training sample corresponds to a distinct reservoir scenario. The scenarios differ in well operations (such as injection pressure, water cut limits, and operational regime), in reservoir geometry, in fluid properties, in the permeabilities and porosities of the individual layers of the model, and in saturation bounds. A naive grid sweep over so many parameters is infeasible. Random uniform sampling is feasible but suffers from poor coverage of the joint parameter space.

The dataset was therefore constructed by Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) over the joint parameter space. LHS partitions each dimension into equiprobable intervals and selects one point per interval before applying independent random permutations across dimensions. This procedure guarantees uniform marginal coverage and substantially better joint coverage than independent uniform sampling at the same sample budget, a standard result in design of experiments. For parameters whose physically meaningful range spans several orders of magnitude, such as absolute permeabilities and solver tolerances, the sampling is performed on a logarithmic scale to ensure adequate coverage of small and large values alike.

Each candidate scenario produced by the sampler is subjected to a set of physical validity checks before being passed to the simulator. These checks reject scenarios that violate the modeling assumptions discussed above, for example scenarios in which the simulated time horizon is shorter than the required number of snapshot steps, or in which the relationship between injection pressure and total flow rate is physically inconsistent. Rejected scenarios are discarded silently and do not enter the dataset. The complete list of sampled parameters, their ranges, and the validity checks is provided in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

Within each scenario, the temporal evolution of the reservoir is recorded as a sequence of snapshots at fixed intervals of simulation time. Each snapshot is treated as an independent training sample. This choice avoids autoregressive error accumulation and matches the design of a spatial super-resolution model, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:modern_frontier}. The dynamic scalars associated with each snapshot, including time, cumulative water cut, total flow rate, and bottom-hole pressure, are stored alongside the spatial fields and serve as the conditioning vector in the third experiment described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology_conditioning}.

At every snapshot, the thermodynamic state of the reservoir is encoded as a three-channel tensor. The first channel carries the global pressure field, the second channel carries the water saturation in the fracture network, and the third channel carries the water saturation in the porous matrix blocks. This dual-saturation representation reflects the dual-porosity nature of the modeled medium: the fracture saturation governs the rapid convective fronts, while the matrix saturation governs the slower elastic fluid exchange. The two saturation channels share the same spatial geometry but evolve on different time scales, and reconstructing them jointly is the multi-task component of the super-resolution task.

Because the three state variables operate on vastly different numerical scales, a normalization step is required before training. The pressure field varies over an interval of several megapascals, while the saturation fields are bounded between physical irreducible and maximum values within the unit interval. Standard score normalization, in which each channel is shifted by its mean and scaled by its standard deviation, would mix the magnitude information across channels and would not respect the bounded nature of the saturation variables. The present work therefore adopts a channel-wise normalization scheme, fitted on the training split, that preserves the relative magnitude of pressure gradients and respects the physical bounds of the saturation channels. The exact normalization formulas, the statistics computed on the training split, and the treatment of strictly positive quantities through logarithmic transformation are documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

Finally, the dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test splits at the scenario level rather than at the snapshot level. Splitting at the snapshot level would place snapshots of the same scenario into different splits, leaking information across the partition. Splitting at the scenario level guarantees that the test split contains entirely unseen reservoir configurations, which is the relevant generalization regime for the surrogate model. The split is reproducible across runs by seeding the underlying shuffling procedure, so that the training, validation, and test populations of every reported experiment are identical.

\section{Pixel-Loss Regression Baselines}
\label{sec:methodology_baselines}

The first experiment establishes pixel-loss regression baselines. These baselines play two roles in the wider methodology. They quantify the best point-wise accuracy that can be obtained on the dataset under purely deterministic optimization, and they serve as the starting point for the adversarial experiment described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology_adversarial} and the conditioning experiment described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology_conditioning}. The methodological decision in this stage concerns the choice of architectural family and the choice of training objective.

The first methodological decision concerns the choice of architectural family. The literature review identified residual convolutional architectures as the established choice for spatial super-resolution of structured physical fields, since they preserve the spatial resolution of features through the trunk and apply sub-pixel upsampling only at the network tail. Within this family, three architectures were retained for systematic evaluation: MSRResNet, MDSR, and RFDN, all discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:dl_foundations}.

These three architectures span the relevant design axes for the present problem. MSRResNet is a moderate-depth residual generator that serves as a lightweight reference and as the backbone of the subsequent adversarial experiment. MDSR is a deeper residual model whose multi-scale design allows feature reuse across upscaling factors, although the present work uses only one fixed upscaling factor; the deeper trunk is retained as a higher-capacity baseline. RFDN is a lightweight architecture based on information distillation, included to test whether a smaller model with explicit feature distillation can match the deeper backbones on the dual-porosity task. All three architectures omit batch normalization in the residual trunk, in line with the conclusions of Lim et al.~\cite{lim2017enhanced} that batch normalization distorts the absolute magnitudes of continuous physical fields.

Architectures based on attention, recurrence, or diffusion, discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:modern_frontier}, were not retained as baselines, since each of them violates at least one of the constraints of the target surrogate, namely a single deterministic forward pass on consumer hardware within the interactive inference budget.

For the regression baselines, the training objective combines a smooth point-wise term with no auxiliary perceptual or adversarial signals. The Charbonnier loss is adopted as the point-wise term, defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}}(\hat{X}, X) = \sqrt{(\hat{X} - X)^2 + \varepsilon^2},
    \label{eq:charbonnier}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{X}$ denotes the reconstructed field, $X$ the reference field, and $\varepsilon$ a small positive constant. The Charbonnier loss behaves as an $L_1$ penalty for large errors and as a smooth quadratic penalty near zero. The smooth behaviour near zero stabilizes the gradient at small errors compared to a strict $L_1$ loss, and the linear behaviour away from zero limits the influence of outliers compared to a mean squared error. The value of $\varepsilon$ adopted in the experiments is documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

The role of the pixel-loss baselines in the wider methodology is to establish a numerical reference for point-wise quality and to provide pre-trained weights for the adversarial and conditioning experiments. They are not expected to perform best on structural or spectral criteria, since the limitation discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:gan_revolution} applies: the minimizer of a point-wise loss is the conditional expectation of the target distribution, which acts as a deterministic spatial average and smooths out sharp displacement fronts.

Alongside the three learned baselines, a separate hold-out comparison is performed against bicubic interpolation, treated as a deterministic baseline that does not require training. This comparison is methodologically important: if a learned regression model failed to outperform bicubic interpolation on the dual-porosity task, then the entire surrogate approach would be invalidated, regardless of the subsequent adversarial or conditioning experiments. Bicubic interpolation therefore acts as a sanity check rather than as a competitor to the neural surrogates.

\section{Adversarial Training with Physics-Informed Regularization}
\label{sec:methodology_adversarial}

The second experiment introduces the adversarial training framework and the physics-informed regularization term. The methodological decisions in this stage concern the role of adversarial training in the overall objective, the structure of the perceptual criterion, and the design of the physics-informed regularizer.

The adversarial framework is introduced not as a stand-alone training paradigm but as a corrective signal layered on top of pixel-loss optimization. The pixel-loss regression baseline produces reconstructions that are accurate on average but systematically smoother than the ground truth. The role of the adversarial component is to penalize this systematic smoothing by training a discriminator that distinguishes between the smoothed surrogate output and the true high-resolution state. This formulation is consistent with the conclusions of Ledig et al.~\cite{cvf_srgan} and Wang et al.~\cite{wang2018esrgan} that adversarial training shifts the network output from the conditional mean of the target distribution towards the manifold of plausible high-resolution states.

To make this corrective role explicit, the adversarial experiment is initialized from the pixel-loss baseline rather than from random weights. Starting from random weights typically leads to instability and to mode collapse, in which the generator converges on a small set of patterns that satisfy the discriminator but disregard the input. Initializing from a pixel-loss baseline ensures that the generator already produces a physically reasonable approximation of the target field at the beginning of adversarial training, and the adversarial signal subsequently refines the high-frequency content. The discriminator is trained from scratch and is updated at a rate consistent with stable adversarial dynamics, with details documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

\subsection{Perceptual criterion adapted to physical fields}
\label{subsec:perceptual}

A standard adversarial framework for natural images couples the discriminator objective with a perceptual loss computed in the feature space of a pre-trained classifier such as VGG. This choice is inappropriate for the present setting for two reasons. First, the pre-trained classifier is trained on natural color images and its feature space is not calibrated for continuous physical fields such as pressure and saturation. Second, the dual-porosity state tensor has three channels whose physical meaning does not match the RGB convention.

The present work therefore adopts a perceptual criterion that operates directly on the physical field rather than on a learned feature space. The criterion combines two terms. The first term penalizes differences in the magnitude of spatial gradients between the reconstructed and reference fields. Spatial gradients are computed by convolving each channel with discrete Sobel kernels, which produce the horizontal and vertical components of the field gradient. The penalty is then applied to the difference of the gradient magnitudes. This term enforces the recovery of sharp displacement fronts without being overly sensitive to small spatial misalignments. The second term penalizes differences in the Fourier amplitude spectrum of the reconstructed and reference fields. The Fast Fourier Transform maps the spatial field onto a frequency basis, and the penalty is applied to the difference of the absolute Fourier coefficients. This term enforces the recovery of the high-frequency spectral content that pixel-loss optimization systematically suppresses, as documented in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}.

The combined perceptual criterion is generic with respect to the physical meaning of the channels and does not rely on any external pre-trained network. This property is essential for transferability of the method to reservoirs with different physical regimes, since no domain-specific perceptual model is required.

\subsection{Physics-informed regularization}
\label{subsec:physics_reg}

The adversarial framework alone improves the structural sharpness of the reconstruction but does not enforce any constraint specific to the physics of Type~II fractured-porous media. To address this limitation, a physics-informed regularization term is added to the generator objective. The regularization combines two complementary physical criteria, derived from the governing system of Section~\ref{sec:theoretical_foundations}.

The first criterion targets the diffusive structure of the pressure channel. The pressure field is governed by a parabolic equation, which produces locally smooth spatial profiles in the absence of strong heterogeneity. Excessive local curvature in the reconstructed pressure field indicates the presence of non-physical artefacts introduced by the adversarial dynamics. The criterion penalizes the magnitude of a discrete Laplacian applied to the reconstructed pressure channel:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{press}} = \big\lVert \Delta_h \hat{P} \big\rVert_1,
    \label{eq:press_smoothness}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{P}$ denotes the reconstructed pressure channel, $\Delta_h$ the discrete Laplacian operator, and $\lVert \cdot \rVert_1$ the mean absolute value over the spatial domain. This term acts as a smoothness prior on the pressure channel, while leaving the saturation channels untouched.

The second criterion targets the geometric coherence of the saturation fronts. In Type~II fractured-porous media, the saturation channels exhibit sharp displacement fronts that separate swept and unswept regions, and the locus of strong gradient magnitude indicates the position of these fronts. The criterion penalizes the discrepancy between the gradient magnitudes of the reconstructed and reference saturation channels:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{front}} = \sum_{c \in \{S, \bar{S}\}} \Big\lVert \, \big|\nabla \hat{X}_c\big| - \big|\nabla X_c\big| \, \Big\rVert_1,
    \label{eq:front_consistency}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{X}_c$ and $X_c$ denote the reconstructed and reference saturation channels, and $|\nabla \cdot|$ denotes the magnitude of the spatial gradient computed by Sobel convolution. This term complements the perceptual gradient term of the adversarial objective by aligning the predicted fronts with the reference fronts of the saturation channels specifically.

The two criteria are combined into a single physics-informed term
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{phys}} = \lambda_{\text{press}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{press}} + \lambda_{\text{front}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{front}},
    \label{eq:phys_combined}
\end{equation}
with positive scalar weights $\lambda_{\text{press}}$ and $\lambda_{\text{front}}$. The two terms operate on disjoint channels: the pressure smoothness term acts only on the pressure channel and is silent on the saturation channels, while the front consistency term acts only on the saturation channels and is silent on the pressure channel. This separation prevents the two terms from interfering with each other and matches the physical structure of the dual-porosity formulation.

The use of separate channel-wise criteria rather than a single integral conservation criterion follows from a methodological observation. An integral mass-conservation criterion would require accurate knowledge of porosities, cell volumes, and source terms on both the coarse and fine grids, and would be sensitive to the convergence of the coarse-grid solver. The proposed channel-wise criteria operate directly on the reconstructed fields and require no auxiliary information beyond the fields themselves, which makes them applicable across a wider range of scenarios and robust to imperfections in the coarse-grid input.

The generator is trained to minimize the sum of four terms: the pixel-loss term of Equation~\ref{eq:charbonnier}, the adversarial term produced by the discriminator, the perceptual term combining gradient and spectral penalties, and the physics-informed term of Equation~\ref{eq:phys_combined}. The combined objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{G} = \lambda_{\text{pix}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}} + \lambda_{\text{adv}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} + \lambda_{\text{perc}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}} + \lambda_{\text{phys}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{phys}},
    \label{eq:gan_total}
\end{equation}
where the scalar weights govern the relative influence of each component. The role of each weight is methodologically distinct: the pixel weight anchors the reconstruction at the pixel-loss baseline, the adversarial weight controls the strength of the corrective signal, the perceptual weight controls the recovery of sharp structures, and the physics weight controls the adherence to the dual-porosity priors. The specific values adopted in the experiments are documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

A three-phase training schedule is adopted to ensure stable convergence of the adversarial dynamics. During the first phase, only the discriminator is updated, while the generator remains fixed at its pixel-loss state. This phase allows the discriminator to learn a meaningful decision boundary before exerting pressure on the generator. During the second phase, both networks are updated, but the adversarial weight on the generator is held at zero, so that the generator is refined only through the pixel, perceptual, and physics terms. During the third phase, the full objective of Equation~\ref{eq:gan_total} is activated. This schedule is consistent with stability practices reported in the adversarial super-resolution literature.

To further stabilize the adversarial dynamics, the discriminator update is skipped on iterations in which the discriminator loss falls below a fixed threshold, which prevents the discriminator from overpowering the generator. The same effect is also obtained by injecting small Gaussian noise into the discriminator input during the early phases of training, with the noise amplitude decayed over a fixed schedule. These stabilization techniques are documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

\section{Conditioning on Dynamic Reservoir State}
\label{sec:methodology_conditioning}

The third experiment extends the MDSR pixel-loss baseline with feature-wise linear modulation conditioned on a vector of dynamic simulation scalars. The choice of conducting this experiment on the pixel-loss baseline rather than on the adversarial generator is methodological: any difference in reconstruction fidelity observed between the conditioned and the unconditional variants must be attributable to the conditioning signal alone, without confounding interactions with the adversarial dynamics or with the physics-informed regularizer. The methodological decisions in this stage therefore concern the form of the conditioning mechanism, the composition of the conditioning vector, and the experimental protocol used to assess its effect.

Section~\ref{subsec:conditioning} motivated the conditioning experiment by the observation that identical coarse-grid inputs may correspond to several distinct fine-grid realizations, depending on the operational regime of the reservoir. If this observation holds in practice, conditioning the MDSR backbone on a vector that summarizes the operational regime should reduce the ambiguity of the upscaling task and improve point-wise reconstruction fidelity over the unconditional MDSR baseline. If the observation does not hold, then the operational regime is either implicitly recoverable from the coarse-grid input or negligible for the present dataset, and the additional conditioning signal provides no measurable benefit. The conditioning experiment is therefore designed as a falsifiable test of the hypothesis associated with RQ3.

Feature-wise Linear Modulation, introduced by Perez et al.~\cite{perez2018film} and discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:conditioning}, was selected as the conditioning mechanism for several reasons. The mechanism injects scalar information into the residual trunk through an affine transformation of the feature activations and does not depend on the spatial structure of the input. It adds a small number of additional parameters relative to the size of the MDSR backbone, which preserves the computational profile of the surrogate. Crucially for the experiment, FiLM can be initialized as the identity transformation, so that the conditioned MDSR reduces to its unconditional counterpart at the beginning of training. This last property is essential for a fair comparison: both models share the same initial pixel-loss state, and any subsequent difference in performance is attributable to the conditioning signal alone.

The conditioning vector is encoded by a small auxiliary network whose output produces the per-channel scale and shift parameters of the FiLM layer. The encoder is initialized to produce neutral parameters at the beginning of training, and the FiLM layer is initialized to the identity transformation. The exact dimensions of the auxiliary encoder and the placement of FiLM layers in the backbone are documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

The conditioning vector itself is composed of dynamic scalars associated with the current snapshot, such as simulation time, cumulative water cut, total flow rate, and bottom-hole pressure. These quantities are physically related to the operational regime of the well and govern the inter-porosity exchange term of the dual-porosity formulation, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:theoretical_foundations}. Each scalar is normalized using the same per-statistic procedure applied to the spatial fields, ensuring that the components of the conditioning vector operate on comparable numerical scales.

Two ablation configurations are considered. The minimal configuration includes only the simulation time and the cumulative water cut, which carry the coarsest information about the operational regime. The extended configuration adds the total flow rate and the bottom-hole pressure, which carry finer information about the instantaneous state of the well. The unconditional baseline, in which no FiLM layer is active, is compared against both configurations. The role of the ablation is to determine which subset of the dynamic scalars, if any, carries information that the network cannot recover from the coarse-grid spatial input alone.

\section{Comparative Analysis Plan}
\label{sec:methodology_comparative}

The three experiments described above are connected by a comparative analysis plan that integrates their outcomes. The plan specifies the comparisons performed in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} and the methodological role of each comparison.

The pixel-loss baselines and the adversarial models are linked by a pre-training pipeline. The pixel-loss baseline of the selected backbone is trained first, and its converged weights are reused as the initialization of the corresponding adversarial generator. The discriminator is always trained from scratch. The conditioning experiment is conducted on the MDSR backbone alone, without adversarial refinement: the unconditional MDSR baseline of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_baselines} is compared with conditioned MDSR variants trained under the same pixel-loss objective and with FiLM layers initialized as the identity transformation. This shared starting point isolates the effect of each methodological choice and supports a fair comparison.

Building on this pipeline, four comparisons are performed in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}, each tied to a research question. Comparison~A contrasts the three pixel-loss regression backbones against each other and against bicubic interpolation, in order to identify the most appropriate regression baseline. Comparison~B contrasts the best pixel-loss baseline against its adversarial counterpart trained with the full objective of Equation~\ref{eq:gan_total}, in order to quantify the perception-distortion trade-off addressed by RQ1. Comparison~C contrasts the adversarial model with and without the physics-informed regularization term of Equation~\ref{eq:phys_combined}, in order to isolate the contribution of the physics-informed component addressed by RQ2. Comparison~D contrasts the unconditional MDSR pixel-loss baseline with the two conditioned MDSR variants described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology_conditioning}, in order to test the hypothesis associated with RQ3 in isolation from the adversarial framework. The metrics used in each comparison are those introduced in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}, and the specific evaluation protocol is documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

The deployed surrogate is selected by an aggregate criterion that combines point-wise, structural, spectral, and physics-informed metrics. The criterion gives preference to models that achieve high structural and spectral fidelity without an excessive degradation of point-wise accuracy, consistent with the perception-distortion trade-off framing of RQ1. A model is retained for deployment only if it outperforms bicubic interpolation on every metric, which guarantees that the learned surrogate is preferable to the deterministic baseline regardless of the metric chosen by the downstream user. The numerical thresholds adopted for the aggregate criterion, and the resulting selection, are reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

To ensure that the comparisons performed in this work are reproducible, every experiment fixes the random seeds of the underlying frameworks, freezes the data split at the scenario level, and stores the normalization statistics on disk so that the training, validation, and test populations are identical across runs. Pre-trained pixel-loss checkpoints are reused without modification across all derived adversarial and conditioning experiments. The exact software environment, including the version of each library and the format of the stored artefacts, is documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:methodology_summary}

This chapter formalized the experimental methodology used in the present work. The dataset is constructed from independent coarse-grid and fine-grid solutions of a Type~II fractured-porous reservoir model, sampled by Latin Hypercube Sampling over the joint parameter space and partitioned at the scenario level. Three lines of investigation are pursued. The first establishes pixel-loss regression baselines on three residual super-resolution architectures and quantifies the limits of point-wise optimization. The second adds adversarial training, a physics-aware perceptual criterion, and a physics-informed regularization term derived from the diffusive structure of the pressure field and the geometric coherence of the saturation fronts. The third extends the MDSR pixel-loss backbone with feature-wise linear modulation conditioned on dynamic simulation scalars and is reported as an exploratory falsifiable study, conducted independently of the adversarial framework to isolate the contribution of the conditioning signal. The comparisons performed across the three experiments isolate the contribution of each methodological choice and feed into the aggregate selection of the deployed surrogate. The corresponding implementation details, hyperparameters, and software artefacts are reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, and the experimental outcomes are reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.
