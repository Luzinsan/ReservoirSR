\section{Fundamentals of Subsurface Fluid Dynamics}
\label{sec:theoretical_foundations}

Reservoir management is fundamentally a decision-making process constrained by significant geological uncertainty. As formulated by da Cruz and Deutsch~\cite{pangea_uncertainty}, the optimization of development plans, ranging from the selection of well locations to the definition of production schedules, relies heavily on the predictive capability of subsurface models. However, these models are inherently limited by the sparse sampling of the reservoir interior. Hard data are typically available only at wellbore locations, which represent a negligible fraction of the total reservoir volume. Consequently, the reliability of production forecasts depends on the accurate characterization of the porous medium and the rigorous application of physical laws governing fluid transport within it. To define the scope of the super-resolution task addressed in this thesis, one must first establish the theoretical basis of petroleum geology and the mathematical formulation of multiphase flow.

The hydrocarbon reservoir is defined as a porous geological formation capable of storing and transmitting fluids. From a petrophysical perspective, the state of the reservoir is parameterized by two fundamental scalar fields which serve as the primary inputs for any hydrodynamic calculation~\cite{ssdynamics_guide}. The first parameter is porosity. This dimensionless quantity, typically denoted by $\phi$, characterizes the storage capacity of the medium and ranges from values below 0.05 in tight formations to over 0.30 in high-quality sandstones. The second parameter is absolute permeability, which quantifies the ability of the porous medium to transmit fluids under a potential gradient. Mathematically, this property is represented by a second-rank tensor $K$, describing the conductivity of the interconnected pore network. A critical distinction exists between these two properties: while porosity often exhibits gradual spatial variations, permeability is characterized by extreme heterogeneity, varying by orders of magnitude over short spatial intervals. This spatial variability is not merely a stochastic noise but a result of deterministic depositional processes. As detailed in recent reviews of rule-based modeling~\cite{subsurfaceai_heterogeneity}, reservoirs exhibit complex architectural elements such as high-permeability channels, low-permeability shale drapes, and inclined heterolithic stratification. These sub-seismic features, which are structures smaller than the typical 10-meter resolution of seismic surveys, exert a first-order control on reservoir connectivity and sweep efficiency. Conventional geostatistical approaches that treat heterogeneity as random Gaussian fields often fail to capture these deterministic structures, leading to connectivity bias. Therefore, an accurate predictive model must explicitly account for these high-frequency geological features to correctly resolve the non-linear dynamics of fluid displacement.

The structural configuration of the reservoir further dictates the fluid flow regime. Following the standard classification presented in fundamental literature~\cite{studfile_types}, reservoirs are categorized into distinct morphological types. Layered reservoirs represent the medium as a stack of strata where properties vary vertically but remain relatively constant laterally; flow in such systems is dominated by high-permeability layers. Naturally fractured reservoirs consist of a dual-porosity system containing a low-permeability matrix that stores fluids and a high-permeability fracture network that conducts them. The focus of this research lies precisely on these fractured-porous heterogeneous media. Such systems are characterized by extremely complex spatial distributions of petrophysical properties. In these formations, the macroscopic flow is governed almost entirely by the connectivity of the fracture network, creating highly non-linear flow paths and aggressive displacement fronts that pose significant challenges for coarse-grid approximation.

The dynamics of fluid extraction within these complex formations are governed by specific models tailored for fractured-porous media. Following the classification established in foundational literature~\cite{textbook_fpor}, the focus of this research is on Type II structured reservoirs. In such systems, porous blocks are completely surrounded by fractures. Consequently, the blocks do not form a continuous medium, and macroscopic filtration occurs exclusively through the fracture network. 

To model multiphase flow under these conditions, a single-level mathematical formulation is employed. This approach assumes instantaneous saturation equilibrium within the small matrix blocks and local pressure equilibrium between the blocks and the adjacent fractures. Consequently, the thermodynamic state of the system is entirely characterized by three continuous scalar fields. These include a single global pressure field denoted by $P$, the water saturation within the highly conductive fracture network denoted by $S$, and the water saturation contained within the porous matrix blocks represented by $\bar{S}$.

Unlike conventional porous media, the cross-flow of fluid between the matrix blocks and the fractures is predominantly driven by elastic forces rather than capillary pressure gradients. The intensity of this inter-porosity fluid exchange, denoted by $q_\Sigma$, is directly proportional to the temporal derivative of the global pressure according to established theory~\cite{textbook_fpor}:
\begin{equation}
    q_\Sigma = -\bar{\beta} \frac{\partial P}{\partial \tau},
    \label{eq:mass_transfer}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\beta}$ defines the elastic capacity coefficient of the matrix blocks and $\tau$ represents continuous time. 

The macroscopic transport through the fracture network is governed by an extension of Darcy law. Within the Type II reservoir framework, the total filtration velocity vector is defined by the following constitutive relationship:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{V} = - \frac{K K^*}{\mu_1} \nabla P.
    \label{eq:darcy_fpor}
\end{equation}
In this expression, the absolute permeability tensor of the fracture system is denoted by $K$, while $K^*$ represents the effective phase mobility function and $\mu_1$ is the reference fluid viscosity. By coupling this convective flow with the elastic fluid drainage from the matrix blocks, the complete hydrodynamic process is mathematically formulated as a system of non-linear partial differential equations~\cite{textbook_fpor}:
\begin{align}
    \beta \frac{\partial P}{\partial \tau} + \text{div} \mathbf{V} &= q_\Sigma, \label{eq:fpor_pressure} \\
    m \frac{\partial S}{\partial \tau} + \beta_1^* S \frac{\partial P}{\partial \tau} + \text{div}(f \mathbf{V}) &= \lambda q_\Sigma, \label{eq:fpor_frac_sat} \\
    \bar{m} \frac{\partial \bar{S}}{\partial \tau} + \bar{\beta}_1^* \bar{S} \frac{\partial P}{\partial \tau} &= -\lambda q_\Sigma. \label{eq:fpor_block_sat}
\end{align}
In this system, the effective porosities of the fractures and the matrix blocks are given by $m$ and $\bar{m}$ respectively. Furthermore, the fractional flow function $\lambda$ determines the phase composition of the fluid being transferred between the media. This governing system exhibits extreme mathematical non-linearity. A primary source of this behavior is the definition of relative phase permeabilities. While permeability in the fractures often demonstrates a linear dependence on saturation, the permeability within the matrix blocks follows a complex cubic relationship governed by inherent capillary effects~\cite{textbook_fpor}.

A critical phenomenon arising from the solution of these non-linear equations is the hydrodynamic instability known as viscous fingering. In the context of fractured-porous media, this instability is severely exacerbated. As detailed in fundamental studies of unstable displacement~\cite{istina_fingering}, this occurs when a highly mobile fluid, such as low-viscosity water, displaces a highly viscous oil. The mobility ratio, defined as the ratio of the displacing phase mobility to the displaced phase mobility, serves as a key indicator of stability. When this ratio exceeds unity, small perturbations at the displacement front tend to grow rather than decay. Consequently, the displacing water aggressively penetrates the reservoir through the highly conductive fracture network, forming irregular and rapid finger-like patterns. This accelerated fracture channeling leaves significant volumes of oil bypassed and trapped within the low-permeability matrix blocks, leading to premature water breakthrough at production wells. From a modeling perspective, capturing these fine-scale structures presents a severe challenge. The fingers are often narrow features driven by the local interplay of permeability and pressure gradients, meaning that accurately resolving them requires high-resolution spatial discretization. Coarse numerical grids, typically employed to reduce computational costs, introduce numerical diffusion that artificially smears out sharp gradients. This truncation error suppresses the physical instability and results in overly optimistic recovery forecasts. Thus, the accurate simulation of viscous fingering necessitates a grid resolution that is often computationally prohibitive for full-field models.




\section{Numerical Modeling and Challenges}
\label{sec:numerical_challenges}

The system of coupled partial differential equations governing multiphase flow described by Equations~\ref{eq:fpor_pressure} through~\ref{eq:fpor_block_sat} generally admits no analytical solution for heterogeneous reservoirs due to the non-linearity of relative permeability functions and complex boundary conditions. Consequently, the industry standard for predictive modeling relies on numerical approximation methods, primarily the Finite Difference Method (FDM) or Finite Volume Method (FVM).

As established in the fundamental theory of reservoir simulation~\cite{peaceman1977fundamentals}, the continuous spatial domain is discretized into a grid of discrete elements. In the widely adopted block-centered grid formulation, the reservoir petrophysical properties and thermodynamic state variables are averaged over the discrete volume of each spatial grid block. The transition from the continuous differential operator to a discrete algebraic system is achieved by approximating partial derivatives using finite difference quotients derived from Taylor series expansions. For a generic scalar function $u(x)$, the first spatial derivative can be approximated using a central difference scheme:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \approx \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x - \Delta x)}{2\Delta x},
    \label{eq:central_diff}
\end{equation}
where the spatial grid spacing is represented by $\Delta x$.

However, this discretization process introduces an inherent error known as the local truncation error, denoted by $\epsilon_L$. According to Taylor's theorem, replacing the differential operator with a finite difference quotient results in a mathematical remainder. For a second-order central difference approximation, Peaceman~\cite{peaceman1977fundamentals} defines the error order as:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_L = \mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t),
\end{equation}
where the discrete time step is given by $\Delta t$. This mathematical relationship highlights a critical dependency: the accuracy of the simulation is fundamentally bound to the resolution of the grid. To reduce the approximation error by a factor of four, the grid spacing must be halved. In a three-dimensional model, this geometrically increases the total number of grid cells $N$ by a factor of eight. Since the computational complexity of solving the resulting linear systems scales super-linearly with $N$, high-fidelity modeling becomes prohibitively expensive.

The complexity of the computational problem is further exacerbated when generalizing these concepts to diverse geological formations. While the Type II model isolates macroscopic flow entirely to the fracture network, generalized dual-permeability models, such as those developed by Konyukhov et al.~\cite{konyukhov2019numerical, volkov2016numerical}, describe simultaneous filtration in both the porous matrix and the fractures (Type I formations). The discrete analogue of the pressure equation for such highly conjugated systems takes the form of a parabolic equation approximated by implicit difference schemes:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_3 (\alpha_T + \bar{\alpha}_T) \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} \approx \text{div} \left( (\text{grad } P + \rho g) \right).
    \label{eq:pressure_discrete}
\end{equation}
In this formulation, following Konyukhov's original notation (where, contrasting with the conventions established in Section~\ref{sec:theoretical_foundations}, variables with overbars denote fracture parameters while standard variables correspond to the porous matrix), the effective phase mobility functions are represented by asterisks. The presence of the combined permeability tensor highlights the extreme computational load of modeling macroscopic flow in both interacting media. Solving this coupled system requires robust linearization techniques, such as Newton-Raphson iterations, combined with sophisticated matrix solvers. To generate high-fidelity reference solutions efficiently, splitting methods such as Alternating Direction Implicit schemes are often employed. These reduce the multi-dimensional problem to a sequence of one-dimensional problems while maintaining unconditional stability and second-order approximation accuracy. Konyukhov et al. emphasize that efficient simulation of transient processes in such unified systems necessitates the use of parallel computing technologies~\cite{konyukhov2019numerical}.

Despite these algorithmic advances, a fundamental trade-off persists. Capturing fine-scale physical instabilities like viscous fingering requires a grid resolution finer than the scale of the heterogeneity, leading to models with billions of cells. Conversely, upscaling the model to a coarse grid increases the truncation error, effectively introducing artificial numerical diffusion. In fractured-porous media, this diffusion not only smears out sharp saturation fronts within the fractures but also artificially dampens local pressure gradients. Since the fluid exchange between blocks and fractures is governed by the temporal derivative of pressure, this dampening severely corrupts the inter-porosity mass transfer calculations, ultimately compromising the physical validity of the recovery forecast. It is crucial to note that traditional deterministic reconstruction techniques, such as bilinear and bicubic interpolation, fail to address this issue adequately. As highlighted in recent benchmarks~\cite{konyukhov_article}, these methods effectively act as low-pass filters. While they ensure mathematical continuity, they inevitably smooth out the high-frequency geometric details and sharp flow boundaries lost during the coarse-grid simulation. This computational barrier necessitates the development of learning-based surrogate models capable of reconstructing fine-scale physics from coarse-grid approximations.


\section{Performance Metrics for Flow Reconstruction}
\label{sec:metrics}

The quantitative assessment of surrogate models in computational fluid dynamics requires a multi-dimensional evaluation framework. Relying solely on localized error metrics is insufficient for highly non-linear multiphase flows, as these metrics often fail to capture the topological correctness of the displacement fronts. Therefore, a comprehensive suite of metrics is adopted to evaluate point-wise accuracy, spatial connectivity, physical realism, and spectral energy distribution.

The Mean Squared Error and the related Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio serve as the fundamental measures of point-wise signal fidelity. As formalized in classic digital signal processing literature~\cite{gonzalez2018digital}, the Mean Squared Error quantifies the average squared difference between the predicted state tensor and the high-resolution reference across all discrete grid cells. For a reference field $X$ and a predicted field $Y$ containing $N$ computational grid cells, this error is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MSE}(X, Y) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - y_i)^2.
\end{equation}
The Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio transforms this error into a logarithmic scale, expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{PSNR}(X, Y) = 10 \cdot \log_{10}\left(\frac{\text{MAX}^2}{\text{MSE}(X, Y)}\right).
\end{equation}
In this formulation, the parameter denoted by MAX represents the maximum possible value of the signal range. For the physical state variables considered in this research, all input tensors are subjected to a min-max normalization procedure, rescaling global pressure and saturation fields into a bounded unit interval. Consequently, the value of MAX is defined as unity. While mathematically convenient for optimization, optimizing solely for Mean Squared Error treats error visibility strictly as a function of localized magnitude differences. This approach ignores the strong spatial dependencies characteristic of physical flows, inherently acting as a low-pass filter that heavily penalizes slight spatial shifts in sharp fluid fronts. Consequently, point-wise optimization yields overly smoothed state fields that lack physical instability patterns.

To address this structural limitation, the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) was introduced by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2004image}. Although originally developed under the hypothesis of human visual perception, its mathematical formulation is exceptionally well-suited for evaluating the macroscopic flow geometries and spatial connectivity of fluid phases. The index separates the similarity measurement into three independent statistical components calculated over local sliding windows $x$ and $y$: local mean field magnitude $l$, local field variance representing contrast $c$, and structural cross-covariance $s$. These components are formulated as:
\begin{align}
    l(x, y) &= \frac{2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1}{\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1}, \\
    c(x, y) &= \frac{2\sigma_x\sigma_y + C_2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2}, \\
    s(x, y) &= \frac{\sigma_{xy} + C_3}{\sigma_x\sigma_y + C_3},
\end{align}
where the local means are denoted by $\mu_x$ and $\mu_y$, the standard deviations are given by $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$, and their cross-covariance is $\sigma_{xy}$. Small numerical constants $C_1, C_2$, and $C_3$ are included to stabilize the division when denominators are close to zero. Combining these terms yields the specific composite form of the SSIM index:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SSIM}(x, y) = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + C_2)}{(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2)}.
\end{equation}
The index ranges from -1 to 1, where a value of 1 indicates perfect structural identity. For multiphase flow modeling, this metric provides a highly robust evaluation because it penalizes structural distortions, such as the artificial smearing of viscous fingers or the misalignment of flow barriers, far more heavily than uniform magnitude shifts.

While structural similarity provides a macroscopic geometric evaluation, assessing the physical realism of fine-scale instabilities requires deep feature extraction. To this end, the Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) metric was proposed by Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2018perceptual}. To adapt this visual metric for reservoir simulation, the continuous physical state tensors are first normalized into a standard bounded range. The metric then computes the spatial distance between these normalized tensor patches within the deep hidden layers of a pre-trained convolutional neural network, typically the VGG or AlexNet architecture. Although originally trained on visual data, the early and intermediate layers of such networks function as universal extractors of non-linear spatial gradients and sharp boundary features. Consequently, a low LPIPS score mathematically demonstrates that the surrogate model successfully generated physically plausible high-frequency spatial patterns, such as the sharp boundaries of a water breakthrough, rather than purely evaluating aesthetic quality.

Finally, to rigorously quantify the numerical diffusion effect, standard spatial metrics are augmented with Fourier Spectrum Analysis. By transforming the generated spatial fields into the frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform, it becomes possible to compare the spectral energy distribution across different spatial frequencies. The application of spectral analysis to deep learning-based flow reconstruction was notably demonstrated by Deng et al.~\cite{deng2019superresolution}, who proved that adversarial neural networks can successfully recover high-frequency energy density in turbulent flows. In the context of porous media, regression-based models typically exhibit a severe drop in spectral density at high frequencies, reflecting the loss of sharp displacement fronts. Conversely, evaluating the power spectral density provides mathematical proof when a generative model succeeds in matching the spectral profile of the high-resolution ground truth, validating its ability to reconstruct complex physical flow boundaries.

Beyond mathematical and structural fidelity, the physical consistency of the reconstructed fields must be strictly evaluated according to the conservation laws of the porous medium. In conventional porous media, mass conservation between the coarse-grid input and the high-resolution output is defined by the identity of their integral fluid content:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i,j} V_{i,j} \phi_{i,j} S^{SR}_{i,j} = \sum_{k,l} \mathcal{V}_{k,l} \Phi_{k,l} S^{LR}_{k,l},
\end{equation}
where $V, \mathcal{V}$ are the cell volumes and $\phi, \Phi$ represent the porosity fields at fine and coarse scales, respectively.

However, for the Type II fractured-porous media addressed in this thesis, the fluid inventory is distributed between two interacting systems. Following the single-level mathematical model established in Section~\ref{sec:theoretical_foundations}, the total fluid mass in any discrete grid cell is the sum of the fluid stored in the fracture network and the fluid contained within the matrix blocks. To accurately quantify the material balance in such a dual-medium environment, a novel Dual-System Material Balance Error metric is derived. This metric is defined by the squared difference between the combined fluid volumes of both systems across different grid resolutions:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MBE}_{dual} = \left( \sum_{i,j} V_{i,j} (m_{i,j} S_{i,j} + \bar{m}_{i,j} \bar{S}_{i,j}) - \sum_{k,l} \mathcal{V}_{k,l} (\Phi_{k,l} S_{k,l} + \bar{\Phi}_{k,l} \bar{S}_{k,l}) \right)^2.
\end{equation}
In this formulation, variables without overbars denote fracture properties, while overbars represent matrix block parameters. By coupling the conservation laws of both media into a single evaluation criterion, this metric provides a rigorous measure of the thermodynamic consistency of the surrogate model. A near-zero value for this error is a prerequisite for ensuring that the neural network reconstruction adheres to the fundamental physics of dual-porosity filtration.



\section{Evolution of Super-Resolution Models}
\label{sec:dl_architectures}

The mathematical reconstruction of high-fidelity physical state fields from coarse-grid numerical approximations requires predictive models capable of capturing complex spatial hierarchies and non-linear physical dependencies. While multiphase reservoir simulation is inherently a transient and time-dependent process, the continuous temporal evolution of the system can be discretized into a sequence of spatial snapshots. Consequently, within the framework of machine learning, the acceleration of the overall simulation can be formulated as a spatial super-resolution task applied sequentially to each discrete computational time step. To adapt classical computer vision architectures to the transient physics of fractured-porous media, a specific multi-variable tensor representation is strictly required. Instead of standard multi-color images, the thermodynamic state of the reservoir at any given temporal snapshot is mapped into a physical three-channel tensor denoted by $X \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$. These three channels correspond precisely to the fundamental state variables of the Type II dual-porosity model: global pressure, fracture saturation, and matrix block saturation. Convolutional Neural Networks have established themselves as the standard mathematical framework for processing such highly structured spatial data. Their fundamental efficacy stems from the ability to extract hierarchical features through the sequential application of learnable convolution kernels, allowing the network to abstract localized spatial variations into global semantic representations.

\subsection{Convolutional and Residual Networks}
\label{subsec:dl_foundations}

Prior to the advent of deep learning, spatial upscaling in computational fluid dynamics relied almost exclusively on deterministic interpolation methods, such as bilinear or bicubic filtering. While computationally inexpensive, these mathematical low-pass filters fail to inject new high-frequency information, resulting in the artificial smearing of sharp displacement fronts and exacerbating numerical diffusion. 

The application of deep learning to the spatial super-resolution domain was pioneered by Dong et al.~\cite{dong2014learning} with the introduction of the Super-Resolution Convolutional Neural Network. This architecture reinterpreted the classical sparse-coding pipeline as a deep convolutional network, where the non-linear mapping between low-resolution and high-resolution patch spaces is learned end-to-end. The network consists of three distinct operations: patch extraction and representation using $9 \times 9$ kernels, non-linear feature mapping via $1 \times 1$ or $5 \times 5$ convolutions, and final spatial reconstruction. By utilizing a rectified linear activation function, the architecture achieved superior reconstruction accuracy compared to traditional interpolation. However, the model necessitates the preliminary upsampling of the input low-resolution tensor using bicubic interpolation before any feature extraction occurs. This pre-upsampling strategy forces the network to perform all convolution operations in the high-dimensional space. As a result, the computational complexity scales quadratically with the upscaling factor, causing severe computational redundancy and limiting the practical depth of the network to only three layers. Such a shallow receptive field heavily restricts its ability to capture the long-range spatial dependencies characteristic of complex geological fracture networks.

To resolve the computational bottlenecks inherent to pre-upsampling, Shi et al.~\cite{shi2016real} introduced the Efficient Sub-Pixel Convolutional Neural Network. The fundamental innovation of this model was the proposition that all feature extraction layers should operate exclusively in the low-resolution space. To upscale the final feature maps back to the high-resolution domain, the authors introduced a mathematically efficient sub-pixel convolution layer, frequently referred to as the PixelShuffle operator. Instead of relying on predefined interpolation, this layer learns an array of upscaling filters and employs a periodic shuffling operator to rearrange the elements of a multi-channel low-resolution tensor into a single-channel high-resolution output. By shifting the computational burden to the low-dimensional space, the sub-pixel architecture significantly reduced both memory consumption and inference time, establishing post-upsampling as the definitive standard for modern super-resolution models.

While sub-pixel convolution solved the computational efficiency problem, the quest for higher reconstruction accuracy required deeper network architectures to expand the receptive field. However, simply stacking more layers resulted in the degradation problem, where mathematical accuracy saturates and then degrades rapidly. He et al.~\cite{he2016deep} addressed this fundamental obstacle with the proposal of Residual Networks. They hypothesized that it is numerically easier for a network to optimize a residual mapping, formulated as $F(x) := H(x) - x$, rather than the original unreferenced mapping $H(x)$. To realize this, the architecture introduces shortcut connections that perform identity addition, bypassing one or more non-linear convolution layers. This additive formulation allows gradients to propagate directly through the network during backpropagation, effectively solving the vanishing gradient problem and enabling the training of extremely deep networks. Crucially, when adapting the residual architecture for super-resolution tasks, downsampling operations such as average pooling or max pooling are strictly removed. By maintaining a constant spatial resolution throughout the residual blocks, the network avoids the catastrophic loss of high-frequency spatial details required for accurate field reconstruction.

Simultaneously, Kim et al.~\cite{kim2016accurate} proposed the Very Deep Super-Resolution network, significantly increasing the network depth to twenty convolutional layers. Unlike the aforementioned residual architecture, which utilizes local shortcut connections block-by-block, this approach introduced the concept of global residual learning. The internal architecture consists of a straightforward chain of convolutions to extract deep features. However, rather than predicting the final state directly, the network explicitly learns only the global residual field, which represents the mathematical high-frequency difference between the interpolated low-resolution input and the high-resolution ground truth. This architectural expansion extended the receptive field from $13 \times 13$ to $41 \times 41$ grid cells, enabling the model to exploit contextual information from larger spatial regions. Furthermore, Kim et al. introduced adjustable gradient clipping, which allowed the model to achieve rapid convergence using learning rates four orders of magnitude higher than previous shallow networks. To address the spatial reduction issue prevalent in earlier unpadded networks, the authors systematically applied zero-padding before every convolutional layer to preserve the exact spatial dimensions of the feature maps throughout the forward pass. In the context of reservoir modeling, mitigating boundary shrinkage is strictly necessary to preserve physical boundary conditions at the edges of the simulation grid. However, despite these advancements, this very deep network retained the computationally expensive pre-upsampling strategy.

To address the limitations of pre-upsampling while maximizing the benefits of network depth, Lim et al.~\cite{lim2017enhanced} introduced the Enhanced Deep Super-Resolution and Multi-scale Deep Super-Resolution architectures. These models effectively merged the depth of residual networks with the computational efficiency of the sub-pixel post-upsampling operator. A critical architectural modification in these models is the complete removal of Batch Normalization layers from the standard residual block. While batch normalization stabilizes training in classification tasks by standardizing mini-batch statistics, Lim et al. demonstrated that it severely disrupts the absolute range of continuous state variables in regression problems. For physical multiphase systems, where global pressure and local saturations operate on vastly different numerical scales, batch-wise normalization artificially distorts cross-channel physical correlations. By eliminating this normalization, the enhanced architecture maintains absolute magnitude fidelity while saving approximately forty percent of GPU memory during training. To ensure training stability in such a deep architecture without batch normalization, residual scaling with a factor of 0.1 was adopted. Furthermore, the Multi-scale Deep Super-Resolution architecture incorporates scale-specific processing modules at the input and output while sharing approximately 98 percent of its parameters in a common residual body. This design enables the model to reconstruct target fields of various upscaling factors, such as two, three, and four, simultaneously within a unified framework. As highlighted in recent benchmarks on non-linear diffusion problems~\cite{konyukhov_article}, this multi-scale, unnormalized approach establishes a robust and physically consistent baseline for recovering detailed fields in heterogeneous media.

Despite their exceptional architectural efficiency, enhanced residual networks are fundamentally optimized using point-wise loss functions, primarily Mean Squared Error. By minimizing the average magnitude difference between the predicted tensor and the ground truth, these models inevitably suffer from a regression-to-the-mean effect. Consequently, while the multi-scale residual architecture provides a robust structural baseline, the fundamental physical issue of high-frequency texture loss remains unresolved. Point-wise optimization mathematically guarantees that sharp fluid displacement fronts and highly conductive fracture boundaries will be artificially smoothed, confirming the necessity for a radically different optimization paradigm.

\subsection{Generative Adversarial Frameworks}
\label{subsec:gan_revolution}

The reliance on point-wise optimization metrics, specifically the Mean Squared Error, fundamentally limits the capacity of deep residual architectures to reconstruct high-frequency spatial gradients. As established in statistical estimation theory, optimizing a neural network parameterized by $\theta$ to minimize the expected squared difference between the predicted tensor $G_\theta(X)$ and the ground truth $Y$ mathematically converges to the conditional expectation of the target distribution, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    G_\theta(X) = \mathbb{E}[Y | X].
\end{equation}
For highly non-linear spatial processes exhibiting multiple valid high-frequency states for a single low-resolution input, this conditional expectation acts as a deterministic spatial average. Consequently, the regression-to-the-mean effect heavily dominates the reconstruction. The network perfectly recovers the low-frequency topological structures but completely fails to synthesize the sharp spatial boundaries characteristic of complex phenomena, necessitating a radical shift in the optimization paradigm.

To transcend the limitations of deterministic spatial averaging, Ledig et al.~\cite{cvf_srgan} introduced the Super-Resolution Generative Adversarial Network framework. This approach reformulates the reconstruction task from a pure regression problem into a minimax two-player game involving a generative network $G$ and a discriminative network $D$. The generator $G$, parameterized by $\theta_G$, learns to map a low-resolution input tensor $I^{LR}$ to a high-resolution space. Simultaneously, the discriminator $D$, parameterized by $\theta_D$, acts as a dynamic loss function trained to distinguish between the generated tensor $G(I^{LR})$ and the authentic high-resolution ground truth $I^{HR}$. The optimization objective is defined by the following adversarial value function $V(D, G)$:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\theta_G} \max_{\theta_D} V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{I^{HR} \sim p_{train}(I^{HR})}[\log D_{\theta_D}(I^{HR})] + \mathbb{E}_{I^{LR} \sim p_{G}(I^{LR})}[\log(1 - D_{\theta_D}(G_{\theta_G}(I^{LR})))].
\end{equation}
Through this adversarial training procedure, the discriminator penalizes artificial smoothing and mathematically forces the probability distribution of the generated spatial fields to converge toward the true high-frequency distribution of the reference data.

A critical component of this adversarial framework is the complete abandonment of point-wise tensor comparison in favor of structural evaluation within a deep hidden representation. Ledig et al. proposed the Perceptual Loss, which utilizes a pre-trained deep convolutional network, specifically the VGG architecture, as an invariant feature extractor. Instead of measuring differences in the raw data space, the metric computes the Euclidean distance between the activation maps extracted from the intermediate layers of the VGG network. Mathematically, for a given spatial layer $j$ producing a feature map of dimensions $W_j \times H_j$, the perceptual loss $\mathcal{L}_{VGG}$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{VGG} = \frac{1}{W_j H_j} \sum_{x=1}^{W_j} \sum_{y=1}^{H_j} \left( \phi_j(I^{HR})_{x,y} - \phi_j(G_{\theta_G}(I^{LR}))_{x,y} \right)^2.
\end{equation}
Here, the non-linear operator mapping the spatial fields into the deep feature space is denoted by $\phi_j$. This formulation mathematically guarantees the recovery of complex structural patterns and continuous high-frequency boundaries, successfully restoring the topological fidelity of the generated tensor even if the traditional point-wise accuracy metrics degrade.

Building upon these foundational adversarial concepts, Wang et al.~\cite{wang2018esrgan} developed the Enhanced Super-Resolution Generative Adversarial Network, which currently serves as a state-of-the-art baseline for rigorous scientific applications. This architecture introduced two profound structural modifications to stabilize training and improve the recovery of continuous state variables. First, the standard residual units were replaced with Residual-in-Residual Dense Blocks to maximize the network capacity without excessively increasing the parameter count. Second, incorporating the critical insights established by the EDSR architecture discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:dl_foundations}, Wang et al. systematically eliminated all Batch Normalization layers from the generator. As previously demonstrated, avoiding batch-wise standardization is strictly mandatory to prevent the artificial distortion of absolute thermodynamic magnitudes across different state channels. Furthermore, Wang et al. enhanced the adversarial component by implementing a Relativistic average Discriminator. Rather than predicting the absolute probability that a given field is real, the relativistic discriminator $D_{Ra}$ estimates the probability that the ground truth tensor is relatively more realistic than the generated counterpart. The relativistic objective is mathematically expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    D_{Ra}(I^{HR}, I^{SR}) = \sigma(C(I^{HR}) - \mathbb{E}_{I^{SR}}[C(I^{SR})]),
\end{equation}
where $C$ denotes the non-transformed discriminator output and $\sigma$ represents the standard sigmoid activation function. This relative formulation results in significantly sharper spatial gradients and prevents mode collapse during the optimization process.

Despite the unparalleled structural sharpness achieved by adversarial training, generative models inherently introduce high-frequency computational noise that compromises localized signal stability. To resolve the fundamental conflict between the point-wise accuracy of regression models and the structural fidelity of adversarial networks, Wang et al. introduced the mathematical mechanism of Network Interpolation. This technique leverages the fact that both a base regression model $G_{PSNR}$ and an adversarial model $G_{GAN}$ share an identical architectural topology. By continuously mixing the corresponding learned parameters $\theta$, it becomes possible to formulate a hybrid feature extractor. The interpolated parameter set $\theta_G^{INTERP}$ is defined by a continuous scalar coefficient $\alpha$ bounded within the unit interval:
\begin{equation}
    \theta_G^{INTERP} = (1 - \alpha)\theta_G^{PSNR} + \alpha\theta_G^{GAN}.
\end{equation}
This continuous interpolation mechanism allows for the precise calibration of the output characteristics without requiring computationally expensive retraining procedures. By systematically adjusting the interpolation coefficient, one can derive a unified generator that mathematically balances the localized signal stability of the mean squared error optimization with the sharp spatial boundary definition provided by the adversarial perceptual loss. This property provides a highly robust instrument for tailoring the super-resolution architecture to strict scientific requirements where both structural coherence and absolute mathematical precision are strictly demanded.

\subsection{Spatiotemporal and Attention-Based Models}
\label{subsec:modern_frontier}

The continuous evolution of deep learning has introduced architectures that transcend the spatial limitations of standard convolutional networks. While the adversarial framework provides an optimal balance for single-state spatial reconstruction, an objective mathematical analysis of alternative paradigms is strictly required to understand their theoretical applicability and fundamental limitations within the context of transient multiphase flow modeling.

The transition from independent spatial mappings to continuous spatiotemporal forecasting naturally suggests the deployment of recurrent neural networks. Shi et al.~\cite{shi2015convolutional} introduced the Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory network to capture highly non-linear spatiotemporal correlations. This architecture replaces dense matrix multiplications with convolution operators within the recurrent state transitions. The internal memory cell state $C_t$ and the hidden state $H_t$ are updated as three-dimensional spatial tensors according to the following mathematical formulation:
\begin{equation}
    H_t = o_t \circ \tanh(C_t), \quad C_t = f_t \circ C_{t-1} + i_t \circ \tanh(W_{xc} * X_t + W_{hc} * H_{t-1} + b_c),
\end{equation}
where the operator $*$ denotes the convolution operation and $\circ$ represents the Hadamard product. From a theoretical standpoint, this architecture is exceptionally well-suited for modeling fractured-porous media of Type II. Because the inter-porosity fluid exchange function $q_\Sigma$ is directly proportional to the temporal derivative of global pressure, a network maintaining a continuous hidden state $H_{t-1}$ possesses the mathematical capacity to implicitly approximate this temporal derivative without requiring explicit finite difference calculations. Furthermore, as demonstrated by Caballero et al.~\cite{caballero2017real} in the context of video super-resolution, spatiotemporal networks maximize temporal consistency across sequential states. However, the application of recurrent architectures to long-horizon hydrodynamic forecasting introduces a fatal mathematical flaw known as autoregressive error accumulation. When the predicted tensor $\tilde{X}_t$ is recursively fed back into the network to predict $\tilde{X}_{t+1}$, the local approximation error is amplified at each discrete time step. For a transient simulation encompassing thousands of sequential states, this compounded error drift inevitably leads to numerical divergence. Additionally, retaining multiple high-resolution feature tensors in the GPU memory space for every temporal state fundamentally violates the strict memory constraints of lightweight surrogate applications.

To resolve the restricted receptive field inherent to standard convolutional kernels, Vision Transformers deploy global self-attention mechanisms. Liang et al.~\cite{liang2021swinir} successfully adapted the Swin Transformer architecture for high-fidelity spatial restoration tasks. The fundamental mathematical operation of this architecture is the multi-head self-attention evaluated within local windows, formulated as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{SoftMax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d}} + B\right)V,
\end{equation}
where $Q, K, V$ represent the query, key, and value matrices respectively, $d$ defines the scaling dimension, and $B$ denotes the relative positional encoding. Theoretically, the attention mechanism provides an infinite spatial receptive field. This property would be mathematically ideal for capturing long-range hydrodynamic dependencies, such as the instantaneous propagation of a pressure drop from a production well to distant boundary regions through a highly conductive fracture network. Nevertheless, the computational complexity of the attention operator scales quadratically with the number of spatial grid cells $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$, where $N$ equals the product of grid width and height. Although shifted window partitioning reduces this complexity to a linear scale relative to $N$, the dense matrix multiplications associated with projecting physical state tensors into the transformer embedding space result in a massive floating-point operations count. This inherent computational burden renders transformer architectures mathematically incompatible with the requirement of millisecond-level inference speeds necessary for interactive graphical user interface applications.

The most recent advancement in generative modeling is defined by Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models. As detailed by Saharia et al.~\cite{saharia2021image} and further optimized into latent spaces by Rombach et al.~\cite{rombach2022high}, these models generate high-frequency spatial distributions by reversing a predefined Markovian noise process. The inference procedure requires sampling from a learned conditional distribution over $T$ sequential iterations:
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(y_{0:T}|x) = p(y_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(y_{t-1}|y_t, x).
\end{equation}
Unlike generative adversarial networks which frequently suffer from mode collapse and produce a single deterministic output, diffusion models learn the exact data log-likelihood gradient. Consequently, they possess the mathematical capability to sample the entire target posterior distribution. In the context of reservoir engineering, this property makes diffusion models the absolute theoretical optimum for uncertainty quantification and ensemble forecasting, as they can generate multiple equiprobable, topologically diverse fine-grid realizations from a single coarse-grid input. However, the inference mechanism requires evaluating the deep neural network $T$ times for a single temporal state, where $T$ typically ranges from one hundred to one thousand steps. If a full hydrodynamic simulation comprises $S$ temporal steps, generating the complete transient sequence demands $\mathcal{O}(S \cdot T)$ network evaluations. This iterative sampling requirement fundamentally negates the primary objective of constructing a surrogate model, which is the radical acceleration of deterministic numerical solvers.

Synthesizing this architectural analysis reveals a strict mathematical hierarchy dictated by the operational boundary conditions. Recurrent networks theoretically optimize temporal memory, transformers flawlessly resolve global spatial topology, and diffusion models provide mathematically perfect distributional coverage for probabilistic modeling. However, the objective of this research is strictly confined to developing a rapid, deterministic surrogate solver executable on standard consumer hardware. Under these exact constraints, the generative adversarial framework selected in the previous section provides the only viable mathematical compromise. By mapping the coarse-grid input to the high-frequency state manifold through a single forward network pass evaluated in $\mathcal{O}(N)$ complexity, the adversarial architecture guarantees the structural sharpness of physical displacement boundaries while strictly satisfying the computational time requirements of interactive multiphase simulation.


\section{Super-Resolution in Reservoir Engineering}
\label{sec:physics_sr}

The application of super-resolution techniques to subsurface hydrodynamics necessitates a fundamental redefinition of the reconstruction task. While standard computer vision assumes that low-resolution data is a deterministic and downsampled version of a high-resolution original, scientific super-resolution for partial differential equations operates under a different paradigm. As established in recent research regarding the fundamental nature of numerical solutions, the low-resolution input in computational physics represents an authentic realization of the governing equations evaluated on a coarse mesh. Such solutions inherently contain numerical diffusion and lack subgrid-scale phenomena, meaning the mapping learned by the neural network involves recovering lost physical information rather than simply inverting a blurring kernel.

Adversarial optimization has proven exceptionally capable of recovering the fine-scale structures essential for hydrodynamic accuracy. A crucial validation of this approach was provided by Deng et al.~\cite{deng2019superresolution}, who demonstrated that generative adversarial networks successfully restore the high-frequency energy density of velocity fields. To evaluate the unsteady characteristics of dominant scale eddies, the researchers utilized a two-point spatial correlation coefficient of the velocity components, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    R_{vv}(x, y, x_0, y_0) = \frac{\langle v(x, y) v(x_0, y_0) \rangle}{v_{rms}(x, y) v_{rms}(x_0, y_0)}.
\end{equation}
In this expression, the angle brackets denote the ensemble average over multiple samplings. Their results confirmed that while conventional regression models act as low-pass filters that suppress small-scale fluctuations, the adversarial framework restores high-wavenumber content. This spectral recovery allows for the reconstruction of sharp boundaries and instabilities, such as viscous fingering patterns, which are typically lost to the averaging effects of point-wise optimization.

To reconcile these generative capabilities with the rigorous requirements of fluid mechanics, recent studies have proposed hybrid architectures that incorporate physical constraints as regularization terms in the loss function. A prominent example of this approach is the development of the super-resolution constrained generative adversarial network by Doloi et al.~\cite{arxiv_doloi_srcsgan}. Addressing the problem of upscaling oil saturation maps, the authors identified that standard adversarial training does not guarantee mass conservation. Specifically, the total mass of fluid calculated from the reconstructed high-resolution field often deviates from the mass present in the low-resolution input, leading to material balance errors that are unacceptable for engineering applications. To resolve this, they introduced a mass balance loss term which penalizes the squared difference between the total fluid mass in the input and output fields, computed by integrating saturation over the pore volume:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{mass} = \left( \sum_{i,j} \phi_{i,j} V_{i,j} S^{SR}_{i,j} - \sum_{k,l} \Phi_{k,l} \mathcal{V}_{k,l} S^{LR}_{k,l} \right)^2.
\end{equation}
Here, the parameters $\phi, V$ and $\Phi, \mathcal{V}$ represent porosity and cell volume at fine and coarse scales respectively. In their experiments using the SPE10 benchmark dataset, this physics-constrained formulation demonstrated a significant reduction in material balance error compared to unconstrained architectures.

The state of the art in reservoir-specific reconstruction has been further advanced by the integration of multi-objective optimization strategies for heterogeneous media. Aslam, Li, and Yan~\cite{li2025spe} proposed a hybrid multiscale simulation framework designed for high-contrast channelized reservoirs. Their methodology utilizes a comprehensive multi-component loss function to ensure both global accuracy and the preservation of critical flow features at the wellbore locations. The total loss is mathematically formulated as the weighted sum of several sub-terms:
\begin{equation}
    L_{total} = L_{MSE} + \lambda_{well} L_{well} + \lambda_{grad} L_{grad} + \lambda_{TV} L_{TV} + \lambda_{diff} L_{diff}.
\end{equation}
This formulation includes specialized terms for well location accuracy, gradient preservation to maintain sharp flow boundaries, and a total variation regularizer for spatial smoothness. By processing dynamic flow information alongside static permeability maps, this model achieved average root mean square errors of approximately 76 psi for pressure fields and 0.046 for saturation distributions. These results confirm that deep architectures can effectively correct coarse-grid inaccuracies while providing a 16-fold computational speedup compared to fine-scale numerical solvers.

Despite the substantial progress in modeling single-porosity reservoirs, a critical scientific gap remains regarding naturally fractured formations. A rigorous review of the contemporary literature confirms that no published study has addressed the spatial super-resolution of state variables for the Type II dual-porosity mathematical formulation. Current state-of-the-art models are designed to reconstruct a single-continuum state, typically consisting of one pressure field and one saturation field. In contrast, the dual-porosity system requires the simultaneous and coupled reconstruction of three state variables, including global pressure, fracture saturation, and matrix block saturation. The specific challenge of resolving the non-linear mass exchange between fractures and matrix blocks during a spatial upscaling task remains unaddressed. The coupled nature of these variables represents a highly constrained multi-task reconstruction problem that exceeds the capabilities of existing single-medium frameworks. The methodology proposed in this research aims to bridge this gap by extending the physics-informed adversarial framework to a multi-variable tensor representation.
