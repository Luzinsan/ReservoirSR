\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

Capital allocation in the global energy sector has reached unprecedented levels, with total investment exceeding 3 trillion USD in 2024~\cite{iea_investment_2024}. In this high-stakes economic environment, the capacity to accurately forecast the performance of hydrocarbon assets constitutes a primary determinant of project viability and financial success. Numerical reservoir simulation serves as the foundational instrument for such forecasting. By constructing \enquote{digital twins} of the subsurface, engineers attempt to predict production dynamics and optimize recovery strategies decades into the future. However, the application of reservoir simulation is inherently constrained by the complexity of the geological systems it attempts to represent. As noted by Saleri and Toronyi~\cite{spe_recovery_economics}, simulation should not be viewed as a deterministic calculator but rather as a probabilistic tool subject to significant uncertainties and non-uniqueness. The reliability of these models depends critically on their ability to capture the spatial distribution of rock properties and their impact on fluid flow~\cite{ringrose_value_2013}. Ringrose and Bentley emphasize that the primary purpose of a reservoir model is to provide predictive capacity by placing physical properties correctly in three-dimensional space, rather than merely generating visually appealing graphics.

Yet, an important contradiction is at the core of this methodology: the inverse relationship between predictive accuracy and computational expenditure. High-fidelity models, which are necessary to resolve fine-scale geological heterogeneities and complex multiphase flow phenomena, require prohibitive computational resources. Conversely, coarse-grid models, while computationally efficient, often fail to capture critical fluid dynamics, leading to systematic errors in forecasting. This discrepancy between the operational necessity for rapid, reliable forecasts and the limitations of classical simulators creates a significant bottleneck in effective reservoir management.

To mitigate these uncertainties and maximize asset value, the industry has increasingly adopted \enquote{closed-loop reservoir management}~\cite{jansen_closed_loop_2009}. This paradigm shifts reservoir management from a periodic activity to a near-continuous process, requiring the rapid evaluation of numerous scenarios to optimize well controls and update geological models in real-time. However, the practical implementation of this approach varies significantly across the industry, highlighting the scale of the computational challenge. One prevalent strategy, actively pursued by Russian operators such as Gazprom Neft, focuses on integrated digital platforms. As described by Kusimova et al.~\cite{gazpromneft_ml_conference}, machine learning in this context is primarily employed to automate peripheral processes surrounding the hydrodynamic simulator, such as interpreting seismic data or predicting well failures. While this enhances overall workflow efficiency, the core prediction engine remains a classical numerical simulator, leaving the fundamental cost of each individual high-precision forecast unresolved.

A fundamentally different philosophy is observed among Western majors, such as Equinor, which prioritize the management of geological uncertainty through ensemble modeling. Rather than seeking a single deterministic forecast, this strategy involves assessing a probabilistic range of outcomes using data assimilation methods like Ensemble Smoother with Multiple Data Assimilation (ES-MDA)~\cite{equinor_es_mda}. Technologically, this approach necessitates launching thousands of parallel simulations to generate probabilistic production profiles. While effective for uncertainty quantification, the requirement to run such massive ensembles exposes a critical limitation in current computational capabilities.

The computational cost of such ensemble-based workflows is prohibitive when applied to high-fidelity geological models. As highlighted by Hayder et al. in the context of giant reservoir simulation~\cite{stanford_hpc_simulation}, running models with billions of cells requires massive high-performance computing (HPC) infrastructure and can take weeks to complete a single realization. Consequently, to make ensemble calculations feasible, practitioners are often forced to upscale geological models, increasing grid block sizes from the required fine scale (e.g., $10\times10$ meters) to coarse scales (e.g., $100\times100$ meters)~\cite{slb_delfi_ensemble}. This upscaling process introduces a critical trade-off. While it reduces simulation runtime, it inevitably leads to the loss of information regarding fine-scale geological heterogeneities, such as high-permeability channels and flow barriers. As noted in reviews of uncertainty quantification methods~\cite{uq_reservoir_review}, the inability of coarse models to resolve these features results in numerical diffusion and a failure to reproduce complex, unstable flow phenomena like viscous fingering.

Although reduced-order modeling (ROM) techniques, such as Trajectory Piecewise Linearization (TPWL), have been proposed to accelerate these calculations by factors of 100 or more~\cite{rom_review_spe}, they often require computationally expensive offline training and may lack robustness when applied to highly nonlinear multiphase flow problems outside the training regime. Consequently, grid coarsening remains the dominant strategy for practical ensemble modeling. However, recent studies by Pola et al.~\cite{geological_heterogeneity_impact} demonstrate that this strategy is fundamentally flawed for complex recovery processes. Their research indicates that the error arising from numerical dispersion during grid coarsening can be as large as the error resulting from an incorrectly configured physical model. Specifically, coarse grids fail to resolve micro- and meso-scale heterogeneities, which govern the propagation of fluid fronts. From a mathematical perspective, the dynamics of multiphase filtration in fractured-porous media are described by nonlinear parabolic partial differential equations, structurally analogous to the nonlinear diffusion problems investigated in recent super-resolution studies~\cite{konyukhov_article}. This leads to systematically biased forecasts that cannot be corrected by simple parameter adjustment. Thus, a critical gap exists. Traditional deterministic reconstruction techniques, such as bilinear and bicubic interpolation, fail to address this issue adequately. As highlighted in recent benchmarks~\cite{konyukhov_article}, these methods effectively act as low-pass filters, smoothing out sharp discontinuities and unable to recover the high-frequency geometric details of flow boundaries lost during coarsening. Consequently, there is a lack of methods capable of recovering high-fidelity, physically consistent flow details from coarse-grid simulations without incurring the prohibitive cost of fine-grid modeling. Deep learning, specifically super-resolution algorithms, offers a promising path to bridge this gap by learning the mapping between large-scale dynamics and fine-scale physics.

The primary goal of this thesis is to develop and investigate a neural network software complex for the accelerated reconstruction of transient high-fidelity pressure and saturation fields in fractured-porous media using hybrid super-resolution architectures. By applying spatial super-resolution sequentially across discrete simulation time steps, the proposed methodology aims to recover the full temporal dynamics of the multiphase flow.

To rigorously address the stated problem, the experimental framework relies on a synthetic dataset of two-dimensional simulations in fractured-porous reservoirs, where macroscopic flow occurs through a highly conductive fracture network while porous matrix blocks act as fluid sources. Crucially, to avoid non-physical downsampling artifacts and accurately capture numerical diffusion over time, the paired coarse-grid low-resolution states and fine-grid high-resolution states are generated via independent numerical solvers. The continuous temporal evolution of the reservoir is discretized into sequential snapshots. Each temporal state is then formulated as a three-channel physical tensor comprising global pressure, fracture saturation, and matrix block saturation. 

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed models and transcend the limitations of purely visual assessment, a suite of complementary metrics is employed throughout this research. The Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio, commonly referred to as PSNR, is utilized to measure point-wise mathematical accuracy across the computational grid. The Structural Similarity Index, or SSIM, evaluates the preservation of macroscopic flow geometries and spatial connectivity within the reservoir. Adapted for continuous physical fields, the Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity metric, known as LPIPS, rigorously assesses the physical realism of fine-scale fluid displacement patterns. Fourier Spectrum Analysis is introduced to mathematically quantify the recovery of high-frequency spatial gradients, which represent the sharp flow boundaries typically lost to numerical diffusion. Finally, to ensure thermodynamic consistency, a novel Dual-System Material Balance Error metric is formulated to explicitly quantify fluid mass conservation across both the fracture network and the matrix blocks. Based on this robust evaluation framework, the research focuses on answering the following formulated \textbf{Research Questions (RQs)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} What are the quantitative limitations of standard MSE-optimized super-resolution models in resolving high-frequency geological features, and how do these limitations manifest in spectral and gradient-based metrics?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} To what extent can the integration of physical constraints into an adversarial framework mitigate the "hallucination effect" and reduce Material Balance Error compared to unconstrained generative models?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} Can the method of Network Interpolation between distortion-oriented and perception-oriented models provide an optimal balance of metrics that outperforms individual baselines?
\end{itemize}

Based on these questions, the following \textbf{hypotheses} are formulated:

\textbf{Hypothesis 1 (Structural Sharpness and Point-wise Fidelity Trade-off).} The application of an adversarial network framework constrained by perceptual loss successfully recovers the sharp fluid displacement fronts artificially smoothed by conventional regression models. This architectural shift significantly improves structural and spectral fidelity at the inevitable mathematical cost of lower point-wise accuracy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Independent Variable:} Optimization objective, specifically point-wise loss versus adversarial perceptual loss.
    \item \textbf{Dependent Variable:} The measured trade-off between structural or spectral evaluation scores and point-wise error metrics.
    \item \textbf{Population:} Spatial distributions of multiphase flow state variables within the studied computational domain.
    \item \textbf{Relation:} Integrating perceptual optimization forces the generated state fields onto the manifold of physically sharp displacement fronts, resolving numerical diffusion while inherently trading average grid cell accuracy for true geometric connectivity.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Hypothesis 2 (Dual-System Physical Consistency).} The integration of a novel physics-informed loss function, which explicitly couples the mass conservation equations of the fracture network and the matrix blocks, into the adversarial framework significantly minimizes material balance discrepancies compared to unconstrained generative training. This specialized physical regularization prevents the synthesis of thermodynamically implausible fluid states in dual-porosity media.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Independent Variable:} The application of a coupled, dual-system mass conservation constraint within the optimization objective.
    \item \textbf{Dependent Variable:} The quantified Dual-System Material Balance Error across the computational domain.
    \item \textbf{Population:} Spatial distributions of multiphase flow state variables within the studied computational domain.
    \item \textbf{Relation:} Incorporating coupled physical laws acts as a mathematical regularizer that forces the neural network to strictly balance fluid inventories between the dynamic fractures and the static storage blocks.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Hypothesis 3 (Network Interpolation Balance).} The continuous interpolation of network parameters between a regression-based baseline and a physics-informed adversarial model yields a network state that minimizes a composite error function. This interpolated state provides a quantifiable balance among mathematical fidelity, structural sharpness, and physical consistency, improving the aggregate evaluation criterion compared to the isolated baseline models.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Independent Variable:} The continuous interpolation coefficient governing the fusion of model weights.
    \item \textbf{Dependent Variable:} A composite evaluation criterion integrating point-wise, structural, and physical error metrics.
    \item \textbf{Population:} Spatial distributions of multiphase flow state variables within the studied computational domain.
    \item \textbf{Relation:} There exists a definable interpolation range in which the hybrid model retains the localized signal stability of the regression approach while incorporating the high-frequency displacement boundaries resolved by the adversarial framework.
\end{itemize}

To achieve the goal and test the formulated hypotheses, the following \textbf{objectives} have been defined:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Conduct a domain analysis to identify the limitations of coarse-grid numerical modeling in fractured-porous media and review existing neural network approaches to spatial super-resolution.
    \item Implement and evaluate state-of-the-art residual architectures (MDSR, RRDB) using Mean Squared Error optimization to establish a point-wise regression baseline for the 3-channel state tensors.
    \item Implement an adversarial training framework constrained by a perceptual loss function to recover high-frequency structural details and evaluate the perception-distortion trade-off.
    \item Develop a novel physics-informed regularization module by integrating a coupled, dual-system mass conservation constraint into the adversarial objective function.
    \item Implement the Network Interpolation mechanism to mathematically balance the localized stability of the regression baseline with the structural sharpness of the adversarial model.
    \item Perform computational experiments to compare the proposed hybrid methodology against standard baselines using a comprehensive evaluation framework:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Point-wise accuracy metrics:} PSNR, MSE.
        \item \textbf{Structural and spectral metrics:} SSIM, LPIPS, Fourier Spectrum Analysis.
        \item \textbf{Physical consistency metrics:} Dual-System Material Balance Error.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Create a prototype software application with a PyQt-based GUI to demonstrate the practical inference of the best-performing model.
\end{enumerate}
